The console is the instrument

Pick a Set per deck, mix them live, and see who is holding every control. Mixing lives between decks; shaping lives inside one. Every divider drags, because a preview's size is a machine's answer rather than a layout's — and the whole panel follows the room, pastel on white by day and lit on black in a dark hall. The grip in a bay's head says whose size you are setting: a bay with one is yours to size, and a bay without one is the height of what is in it. Both sides of a divider move either way; the grip says which side the number belongs to.

Three columns, and they are named because you address them: a left pane and a right pane, which fold away to give room, and the centre, which is what they give it to. A pane folds by dragging its boundary out: pull it past the narrowest the pane goes and the pane closes, keeping its outer edge — a gutter at the window's own edge where the pane used to be. Drag that edge back in and the pane returns at its narrowest. Nothing is drawn there while the pane is open, because until it is closed the pane's own boundary is where it always was. Each holds a stack of bays — Library, Program, Mixer and the rest — and every bay below says what it is standing on. The middle one is not a third pane on purpose: folding it is not a thing anybody wants, and solo is.

The engine already said which one is the fader

An earlier draft drew gain and opacity as two identical sliders, which threw away a distinction the code states outright:

/// The level the material arrives at. Colour only.
pub gain: f32,
/// The fader across the blend, [0, 1]. The only one of the two that
/// touches what a layer covers.
pub opacity: f32,

They behave differently too: opacity at zero silences under every blend mode, and gain at zero does not silence over — a black card still covers. So this is a channel fader and a trim, as a mixer has always had them. Opacity is the tall control your hand lives on; gain is the small trim above it, set once and left.

The mixer is an L5, and so is a master effect

A master effect looked like a new kind of node. The engine already writes the signature down:

// The L5 node, nested: `[Texture] -> Texture`.

A master effect is that signature with one input; the mixer is the same with several. One kind, not two — what made the mixer look special is that its instance is built in, the third time this shape has come up: L3 has a built-in orbit camera and writable camera procedures, and the merge record describes the built-in L5 exactly as camera describes the built-in L3.

So admitting frame effects is giving L5 a writable form, not extending the algebra. Fan-in is already solved by uses plus edge; a per-deck effect and a master effect become the same node at different points; and the deck count stops being a system constant, since how many inputs an L5 folds becomes a property of the procedure rather than of one built-in shader. Feedback is the exception: reading the previous frame is a cycle, and wants a decision of its own.

What each region is standing on

Mixer

A channel strip per filled track, all visible, nothing that scrolls out of reach mid transition. Four tracks because the compositing shader declares four inputs, and a track nothing fills draws nothing at all — so the strip is a paged list whose length is a number, the header says how long the list is, and it says which page you are on. Three strips above, because the deck the mock is drawing has three slots filled.

The residency chip is the one control that can be showing two things at once. What a deck is doing and what it was asked to do are separate facts: the budget may hold a slot below the request and never above it, so a deck asked to prime with no room sits parked — the request standing, reconsidered every pass, primed the moment there is room. Drawn as allocated and nothing else, that is indistinguishable from a deck nobody asked for anything, and the only way to tell would be to ask again. So the chip rolls part of the way toward the residency it was asked for and falls back, about once a second, and never lands: the settled word is where the deck is, the word coming up behind it is where it is going, and that it keeps falling back is how it says it has not arrived. Deck C is drawn that way above.

A fader with a fade scheduled on it says the same three things, and a track can say one of them better. A transition is armed on the beat grid, so with the default quantum it is due up to a bar after the key that asked for it — and a control that changes something invisible is indistinguishable from a control that is broken. So the knob and the fill go on saying where the fader is, a hairline across the track says where it is going, and the fill keeps setting off toward that line and falling back, once a second, never covering the gap. Deck B is drawn with a fade to 0.80 armed on it. What a capsule could not say and a track can is which way: the mark is a position, read exactly as the knob's own position is read, so the distance to it is the size of the move. The number under the fader stays the value the deck is at, because that is the thing you are about to reach for.

Those two are the only things on this page that move, they move at one rate off one phase, and each moves only while something is pending — a console with nothing outstanding is completely still. The mask's front is the gap: a wipe is a fade on a number this strip has no control for and no room to show, so an armed wipe is the one scheduled move the mixer cannot draw, and it is named here rather than left to be discovered.

It is a control as well as a readout, and the press counts from what was asked for rather than from what it shows. A click asks for the next of the three — live, priming, allocated, wrapping — taken from the residency this deck was last asked for. On a settled deck the two are the same value and the chip behaves exactly as it looks. On a parked one they are not, and that is the case worth knowing: the chip shows allocated and was asked for priming, so a press asks for allocated — which is the prime request withdrawn. Counting from the word on the chip instead would ask for live, and a press meant to take a request back would put the deck on air. It is the same choice the keyboard already makes: w on a parked deck withdraws.

The chip refuses nothing. A pending transition is a thing the surface says, never a lock it holds — what may be asked for is the instrument's to decide, so every way in meets the same wall, and go on air is always one of them.

The mixer has no crossfader

The mix is the channel faders. A crossfader over the same two decks could only ever be a readout — the mark says where those two faders have already put the pair and is never a number of its own, and a third control over the same two numbers is a second way to say one thing. What it would have bought is not bought here: a constrained sum under one hand, two opacities moved together because one grip moves both. That is the two-deck cut, and this is four strips where the fader is the work. Crossfade to the next deck keeps its x: the request is a throw, and a throw is asked for at the keyboard.

Nothing on a strip starts a fade

A hand on the fader is the fade. An opacity moved over a few seconds is what a fade is, and a fader is already the control for moving one — so a capsule asking for the same move would take permanent room on a strip to save a gesture the strip is built around. What it would have bought is the one thing a hand cannot do: land the move on the beat rather than near it. That is worth having and it is not worth a control here, which is why Fade a deck out or in reaches this instrument from everywhere except the panel.

A fade arriving is still drawn, and that is a different thing: the fader carries the hairline and the fill reaches for it, because a move somebody else scheduled is a move you have to be able to see. Take the fader and it is cancelled — the operator wins.

Inspector

n panes, each showing whatever you point it at. Parameters group by where they land, which for most of them is one node, addressed the way a Set is addressed everywhere else (L2:0:amount) so an effect layer's controls need no second idea of where things live. Only what a Set publishes — the reason this is six rows rather than twenty-five.

A control that lands in more than one node is not one of those, and it is not rare. It goes in the group at the top, which is what the rest of this section is mostly about: it is a published control with no address, it moves every node that declares its key, and a Set that declares no interface at all publishes nothing else.

The pane scrolls, and it says how much it is not showing

A deck's node groups come to more than a pane holds — one geometry publishing nineteen knobs is a single group 454 pixels tall, against an Inspector bay that is allowed down to 151.5 — so the pane's body scrolls under its two heads. The showing row and the deck head stay: which deck you are looking at and what its clock is doing are the two things that must never leave the screen, because everything under them is only legible once you know whose they are.

The wheel over the pane is the way in, three parameter rows a notch, and the pointer decides which pane — two panes are two positions and the wheel turns the one it is over. The keyboard's way in is not bound here. A bay's items are walked with the arrows, this bay's items are its panes and a pane's are its node groups, and the operations page names no key for that walk yet; a key invented on this side would be the panel deciding what the page reserved. So there is one route today and it is the pointer's, which is a gap and not a decision.

A pane showing part of itself says so, in the count at the right of its head — 5 of 5 above, which is the Library foot's 5 of 27 read on the groups a pane stacks rather than on the rows a listing holds. It counts what is whole: a group cut by the top or the bottom edge is drawn as far as the pane goes and is not counted, so the number and the picture cannot disagree. That is rule 04 met by the one part of it that is hard — says how much — and it is why the count is groups and not pixels: a bay's items are what an operator counts, and a percentage is a number about a rectangle.

There is no bar down the side, and the count is the mark instead. This console draws no scrollbar anywhere, and the reason is not restraint: a bar you can drag is a control, which owes a row on the operations page and a key and a map line beside it, and a bar you cannot drag is a shape that looks like a control and refuses the hand — which is rule 03 broken by a thing that says nothing about what a click will do because a click does nothing. A group drawn to the pane's edge is the second half of the mark, and it is the half that says which way there is more.

The position is the pane's own, and a resize does not rewrite it. It is the cursor in the library list one bay over: no operation names it, nothing outside the console could be the record of what it is, and it is not part of the arrangement — so saving an arrangement does not carry it and restoring one does not move it. Drag the pane taller and it draws more of what it holds; drag it shorter and it draws less, and what you scrolled to is still there when the pane comes back, exactly and not nearly. A pane that clamped its own position while being drawn would lose an operator's place every time a window got smaller, which is the same slow leak the arrangement refuses one level out.

What this replaces is a pane that drew a group whole or not at all. That rule was honest while there was nowhere to scroll to — a node head with two of its five parameters under it reads as a node with two parameters — but its price was that a short pane drew no knob at all, and a bay whose whole purpose is the knobs cannot answer a small window with an empty pane. With a position to scroll, a part-drawn group is no longer a lie about what the node has: the count says how many are whole, and the rest of the group is one notch away.

A control that names no one node

A published control carries an address or it does not. Addressed, it is one node's and it is drawn in that node's group — L4:0:exposure is a row under L4:0, and the group head is the address. Unaddressed, it is one control over every node that declares its key: exposure published bare is a single knob moving both renderers, with a range narrowed to the part both of them said they still look like themselves over. Two shapes, one interface, and the second has no group below to sit in.

It is also the ordinary case rather than the exotic one. A Set that declares no interface publishes one control per key and nothing else — a bare name is how you reach every declaration of it, so publishing one control per declaration would put two controls called exposure on a desk, which is a shape the instrument refuses outright. So on a Set nobody wrote an interface for, every row on this panel names no one node, and a panel that grouped only by node would draw an empty pane under a headline promising groups.

So they go first, in a group of their own, and its head says what it stands over instead of where it is. ∗ across nodes · 2 nodes is the address a bare name actually has: not a place, but a reach. The rows under it are parameter rows like any other and carry the same mark, so a control the operator can see is a wildcard is one they will not be surprised by later. The head carries no man / sug / auto, for the reason the folded renderers head below carries none: authority is one node's, and this head is not a node.

First rather than last, because on a Set with no interface it is the whole panel and every group below it is empty, and a pane that opens with a column of empty node heads before it reaches a knob is a pane read back to front.

The two arrangements it beats, and what each would have cost. A row in every group it touches draws one control as n rows — and the number in the left-hand column is a MIDI position, so knob 6 would appear twice, anyone counting rows would count more controls than the deck published, and take back beside two of them would be one binding wearing two faces. A fold, closed by default puts the majority case behind a press, which is the one place hiding something quietly costs most.

A wildcard over exactly one node is that node's, and is drawn there unmarked. Nothing is invented by doing so: every node that declares the key is a set the Set itself decides, and where it has one member there is no second group the row could go in. The cost is that the row moves. Give this Set a second geometry that also declares radius and that row leaves L1:0 for the group at the top, having been touched by nobody — which is the honest consequence of one control per key, and is said here rather than discovered on stage.

What no grouping can fix is that a wildcard crosses authority. Authority is one node's; a bare exposure writes both renderers in one motion. So a renderer handed to an agent hands it, through that control, a renderer you kept — rule 06's switch arriving by the back door at the width of a key. What this bay owes is to make the reach visible before the press, and the group head and the row's mark are that. What happens on the write is not this page's to settle: the operation that moves a parameter carries the same optional address the control does, and nothing in the vocabulary yet says what an authority may refuse.

The deck head, and why it is in the inspector

A head is a strip of chips above the thing it heads, and this is the first one that heads a deck: its clock and its fold, over the node groups that are its shape. It is in the inspector because the inspector is where everything about what a deck is lives, and a deck is running material with a time of its own. The mixer could not take it: the mixer holds only what decides how much of each deck reaches the screen, and a strip is 53 pixels wide with nothing spare in it. It sits under the pane's head rather than in it, because the pane's head says which deck is showing and this says what that deck is doing — and because five chips and a deck's name do not fit on one row of a 237-pixel pane.

Sync is one chip that cycles, and the cycle skips. Free, tempo, beat. Tempo scales the rate a deck advances at; beat locks its position, which needs the ability to jump, so it is offered only where the material is closed form and can be evaluated at a moment. Material that accumulates can be run forward and not placed, so beat is skipped rather than shown greyed — and the reason is what the chip says. Tempo is skipped in the other direction, on material that already reads the beat, because scaling its clock as well would make it follow the room twice. Deck A above is on tempo for the first reason and deck B is on beat.

The anchor reads two numbers and asks for one thing, and the offset beside it is the scrub's own value. T128 is the tempo a deck was engaged at, which is what its rate is measured against; B128 +0.25 is that with the deck sitting a quarter beat ahead of the room. A free deck shows neither, because free is the absence of a transport rather than a setting, and a column reading free on every deck would be four words of nothing — so a free deck has no anchor to press either, which costs it nothing, since free is the one mode that never reads an anchor.

Pressing the anchor re-anchors: it asks for the mode this deck is already in. Engaging a mode takes the room's tempo as the reference the material's rate is measured against, so engaging the one that is already running is how an operator says call this the reference tempo — the picture does not move, the rate goes back to 1×, and the scrub goes back to zero with it. It needs no operation of its own; it is the deck's own mode, named. What it needs is a control that can name it, and the chip on the left structurally cannot. A cycle starts at the mode after the one you are on, so the one state it can never arrive at is the one you are in — and the single case where it does land there is material that accumulates and reads the beat, where free is the only mode left and free reads no anchor. That is a fault of the affordance and not a gap in what can be asked for: every control that names a mode outright gets re-anchoring the day it exists, a pad naming one and a call naming one alike, and only a cycle is shut out of it.

Scrub moves by an amount, which nothing else on this panel does — a quarter beat a press, into that same offset, signed and unbounded because a scrub is the one control here meant to go backwards. It is live only where the deck is beat-synced, since that is the only mode that reads the offset, and where it is not the arrows keep their shape and say why. Nothing is shown as a price, because nothing re-runs a history: placing accumulating material would mean running it again from where it started, and that is not an operation this instrument has.

Composite is the deck's, and the renderer chips below are what it turns into a choice. On, a deck's renderers fold into one result and one of them is live; off, they are all overdrawn. A deck publishing a single renderer draws the chip anyway and says that it changes nothing either way, because a deck that grows a second one needs the control already where it was.

Who is holding a control

A bound parameter shows its source instead of a number: a signal, a MIDI control, a sequencer lane, an agent. That readout is where disagreeing with the system begins — a control you cannot see being moved is one you cannot take back — and take back sits right beside it.

A knob is bound to a deck, not to a Set

A MIDI control is learned against the deck and the position in its published interface — knob 3 is knob 3 whatever Set is loaded — which is why the inspector numbers its rows. Binding to a Set's parameter by name would make the mapping a cost you pay again on every swap, and a learn flow exists precisely to make it a cost you pay once. It also settles the crossing: the same Set loaded into two decks is two addresses, because the deck is in the address.

The order is what the Set published — an interface is a list and its order is the author's, which is a small extension of a decision they are already making. Where it published nothing, the order is the Set's own: node by node in the order the nodes run, and inside a node the order its procedure declared them, each key taken where it first appears. That is what the deck above draws — 1 and 2 under L1:0, 3 and 4 under L2:0, 5 under the renderers, and 6 the control that spans two of them. The number follows the interface and not the order the groups are drawn in, which is why 6 is at the top of the pane: a wildcard takes its place among the controls where its key first appears, and it is drawn first because it belongs to no group below. Those are two different questions and the panel answers each in its own terms.

The engine sorted those keys alphabetically until 2026-08-29, and it was right about the thing it was protecting. The keys come out of a hash map, so left alone they come out in a different order on every run, and a console showing its controls in a different order each run is not a console: a MIDI map is worthless against an order that moves. Stable is the requirement. Alphabetical is one way to meet it and it is the wrong one.

It is wrong because the number is read down the pane. Sorted by spelling, this deck's six knobs are amount 1, exposure 2, hue 3, radius 4, turbulence 5, twist 6 — so the left pane reads 2, 4, 5 then 1, 6 then 3, and the one number on this panel whose whole job is to be counted becomes noise. Worse, the order is then an accident of spelling that the Set's author never chose and cannot change without renaming a parameter. Declaration order is the only order that both holds still and means something, and it is the same order the groups are already in.

What it cost the engine was one order kept rather than one derived, and it is paid. Set::published walks the procedures' own parameter lists now and the map of ranges is only looked up. Each node's parameters arrive from its procedure in the order the procedure declares them, and the engine already holds that list per node — it is what a binding is checked against. What loses the order is the map the values are then kept in. So the cost is that the default interface is built by walking those lists in node order and taking each key the first time it appears, in place of sorting the map's keys: no new state, no new thing for a rebuild to restate, and one guarantee added, which is that the numbering does not move when a parameter is renamed at the other end of the Set.

A map is a file — which knob is which belongs to the hardware in the room, so it is saved and recalled per controller and never travels inside a session.

Two focuses, and they do not look alike

The deck selection persists — it is what a key press is addressed to, and it stays put when you tab away into the library. Keyboard focus is transient and goes wherever tab lands. Drawing them the same way would erase which of the two a reader is looking at, so the selection is a solid ring and focus is a dashed one. A third mark on a strip is neither of them: a Set carried out of the library rings the rectangle it is over, in the panel's text ink and round the outside of the strip rather than inside its edge, and it is gone the moment the Set is let go. It is specified under How a Set reaches a deck.

Two ways into the selection and neither of them is a control: 0 to 3, and a press anywhere on a strip that no knob under the pointer claimed. The second is why the strip is the one thing in this bay with no capsule and no track of its own to press — it is the whole column, and what it means is address the keys here. A deck the mixer is not drawing a strip for cannot be selected, and the Library bay's pulldown offers the same decks for the same reason: a mark that named a deck the press would be refused on would be worse than no mark. The two are one count read twice and not one rule kept in two places — the strips are what both of them are counting.

Authority is per node

man / sug / auto on each node head, never a global mode. Manual is yours alone, suggest proposes into the staging lane, auto acts. One of the four properties the whole system is defined by, so it belongs on the face of the panel rather than in a preferences window. A node nobody has spoken for is manual, which is not a placeholder but the only default that is not that global mode thrown for every node by nobody.

Every node takes one, including the ones that look like they would not. The camera takes an authority; so does a field, whose parameters are declared, addressable and yours to ride even though it draws nothing. A node whose knobs something else can move is a node something else can be let at, and that is the whole of the test.

A head that folds more than one node carries no chip, because there is nothing for it to carry. L4 renderers above stands over three nodes. One chip on it would be one of three answers drawn as the answer, and a press on it would set three nodes at once — the switch rule 06 refuses, narrowed from the instrument to a layer but still the same shape, and still unable to say the arrangement that rule calls useful: this renderer is yours and that one is not.

So the chip's place is filled with a reading rather than left empty. man ×2 · sug is what the three under that head are set to. It is not a fourth value — there are three and they are the vocabulary's — and it is not a control; it is a count, the way 3 of 3 in the mixer head is a count. A head that simply dropped its chip would be a control going away without saying why, which is the one thing this panel does not do. A Set with one renderer has one node under that head and the chip is the control it always was, and no fold mark beside it, because a group of one has nothing to open.

The head opens, and that is how a renderer's authority is reached at all. Pressing the mark unfolds the group into one head per renderer — L4:0, L4:1, L4:2, each with its own address, its own chip and its own rows. That is not a second idea of where things live: it is the same node groups the rest of the pane is made of, and the fold is a saving of two heads' height that you hand back when you need an address. The renderers keep their fold by default because the chip row under the head is a choice of one, and that choice is the deck's rather than any renderer's.

The operation is not owed and the route is. Set a node's authority names a deck and a node and exists; what did not exist was a way to name the third renderer while its head was folded, and the fold above is that. The fold itself owes a row on the operations page, beside the other controls that arrange this console rather than the material — and it is named as owed here rather than written from this drawing, which is the way round that keeps a specification a specification. MIDI reaches none of this: a map line carries a deck number, a range, or a word from a closed list, and it cannot spell a node address at all.

The camera is a node, and it is the one with nothing to turn

A camera procedure is a node like any other. It gets a group, an address, an authority chip and rows for whatever it publishes, and nothing about it is special enough to say twice. Neither deck above has one, which is a fact about these two Sets and not a rule.

The rule is the group under it. Every Set has a built-in camera whether or not its files declare one — a node, last among the L3s, called orbit where nobody named it — so L3 addresses something in every Set that exists, and both panes above draw it.

It is drawn with no rows, and not because it has nothing to set. The orbit has six numbers: a radius, a speed, a height, a field of view, and two clipping distances. None of them is a parameter of that node. They are the Set's own, set from outside when the Set is made or loaded and read by the node rather than declared by it — which is why the node publishes nothing, and why a head with a line under it saying so is what a Set's camera honestly looks like from here.

The head is drawn rather than the group omitted, because the head has values. An address, a name, and an authority that a node nobody has spoken for has by default. Leaving it out would make a deck's camera the one part of a deck that the panel where everything about what a deck is lives does not admit exists, and a Set with two cameras would then have one of them appear and the other not, which is worse than both being plain. A control you cannot use says why rather than going away, and a node you cannot reach is the same sentence one level up.

Where an operator reaches those six numbers is: not here, and barely anywhere. There is no flag that aims a camera. A Set file can record two of the six — the radius and the speed — and the other four come back at their defaults, so a camera saved and a camera loaded are not the same camera unless the four were default already. That is a gap this page can name and cannot close.

What is owed is an operation, and it is owed a decision before it is owed a row. The vocabulary names one and leaves what it acts on open, deliberately, because the camera is two things and they are not one operation: the built-in orbit, whose numbers are the Set's and come in through a record, and a camera procedure, whose parameters are written the way any node's are. Which of the two the row means decides whether it carries numbers of its own or should not exist at all. This page specifies what the bay shows and stops there — the group, the head, and the line saying what it waits on — because writing the control from this drawing would be answering that question with a picture.

The beat moves, always

A person does not read a stopped display as current — and on a panel where nothing moves, a panel that has stopped and a panel that is idle are the same picture. So the beat grid is a light that travels: one traverse a bar, every dot as bright as the light is near it, and the two either side of it always adding up to exactly one dot's worth. It is not decoration and it is not spare budget. One dot lighting at a time would say the same thing only between two flips; a light that is somewhere at every instant says it at every instant. The still picture above is a frame of that travel — the instant of a beat, where all of the light is on one dot.

The octave is a person's, and only one half of it is ever live

½ and ×2 move the grid an octave, and they are the one thing about the tempo that no measurement is allowed to decide. The tracker folds every candidate it hears into the window the grid is already in, so a set that started an octave low stays an octave low however long it listens — folding is what keeps a drifting tempo in one octave, and it is exactly what cannot climb out of one. Pressing this moves the grid and the window together, so tracking carries on in the new octave instead of folding straight back. The phase does not move either way: doubling subdivides the beats already there and halving takes every other one, so the beat you can see stays where it is. It is as authoritative as a tap — the run of evidence behind the old octave is thrown away, so the estimates still arriving from it for the next quarter second cannot pull the grid back.

The refusal is drawn rather than reported, because this is one the panel can work out before you press. A move is refused when the result would leave the range the tracker searches, 60 to 200 BPM: outside it there is nothing to lock to, so the next estimate that disagreed would drag the grid back, and a control that undoes itself two seconds later is worse than one that says no. The panel is holding the tempo already, so it can say which press will be refused; a tap that turns out to be a performer still finding the beat can only be reported afterwards, and that is the difference in what the two controls are allowed to promise. Above the row, ×2 keeps its shape and its reason at 128.0, where doubling would ask for 256.

That range is under two octaves wide, so the two halves are never both available — above 120 only ½ is, below 100 only ×2, and between the two neither of them is. It is a control with two faces of which at most one is ever live, which is a thing to know about it rather than a fault in the drawing: the alternative is a single chip whose face changes with the tempo, and a control that renames itself is worse than one that says no twice.

The four things beside it are one group because they need one thing. The latency offset, and which offset it is

Between what a room hears and what it sees. It is signed both ways on purpose: which of the two is late is a property of the room, and a PA delayed to the back of a hall puts the sound behind a projector that had looked slow. Held inside 200 milliseconds either way, which is past any single device and well into the region where a whole beat has been mistaken for an offset. Negative and the picture waits for the music, positive and it leads — and the sign is the half that gets read wrong at two in the morning, so the panel says it in words rather than leaving −15 ms to be interpreted.

It is in the transport beside audio‑in, because that is what it depends on: with nothing attached there is nothing for the picture to be early or late against, and the pill next to it is what says whether there is. Master, and the two levels that are not one level

The staging lane, and the sense in which a candidate waits

There is no regenerate, and there was never going to be one. Generating is a conversation with a model and it happens outside this process — no network and no secrets enter the render process, which is why MCP is the only prompt surface. What crosses the boundary is a node's source, and the operation that takes it is check and write one node's source, which already exists and is already reached by a model. So the lane's first producer is not a new mechanism: it is the write this instrument already performs, with somebody else at the other end of it. The three-step revision — try a parameter, then a range, then a regeneration — only reaches this lane at its third step. The first is write a parameter and lands inside the frame with nothing to stage; the second has no cheaper path here than the third, because a declared range is a line in the source and changing one is a write like any other.

What that costs the sentence above: a candidate does not wait. A write is checked, written, built on a worker and swapped in at a frame boundary; nothing anywhere holds a finished build out of the picture. So the candidate for a node is what that node is playing, and the lane is an A/B over time rather than over space — you are looking at the new one and the one it replaced is one press away. That is by design and not a shortfall: regeneration is destructive in a slot and safe in the library, because every version that compiled is put into the store under its own content address on the way past, whether or not it stayed on screen. A rejected one costs nothing is that fact and nothing more romantic.

Whether it is on screen: landed, rolled back for costing too much, or refused, which is find out what a write did read against one node — a candidate you cannot see is not one you can judge. And when it arrived, formatted by the host and handed in, the way every other derived value in this panel is.

What the lane draws today is three of those four, and the third of the three is not the node. A verdict arrives from the engine as a swap event, and a swap event is about a build: a rebuild restates every node of a slot, so what the engine can say is this slot's new material landed and never this node's did. So the row is addressed by the deck it landed on — the same A to D the preview cells carry, and the only thing naming a deck — and what it carries beside that is the build's own name and the verdict. The node is omitted rather than approximated, and the letter is not standing in for it: the address a verdict has is the slot, and the finer one is a derivation nobody has written. It is derivable, and nearer to hand than that sounds — a build reports a hash per node, and consecutive builds differ where the hashes do. Both halves of that are this program's already. Every watcher is handed the store it puts its sources in, and says what it put: a build's report is every node of the restated slot with its layer, its index and the address its source went in under — the whole stack rather than the one file that changed, because a rebuild restates the slot. The channel that carries it is neither silent nor unread; it is drained where a build lands, and what comes off it is written down as what that slot is playing, with the version it displaced kept beside it, because a rollback is the only thing that can name what it brings back. So the two lists a diff would read are both in this process, one frame apart. That is a derivation nobody has written rather than a channel nobody opened, and the command-line player is standing in the same place rather than a step ahead of this one. A Set has two forms, and loading one is packaging it

A 16:9 view filling a wide centre column would be 763 pixels tall and eat the inspector whole. So you drag its height and it letterboxes into the width it has — which is what the engine already does. Height given back is rows in the library and the inspector, which are the two bays that take it.

The bay is two regions and they fold apart. The picture is a sink, listed in Outputs as program view, and it is on screen exactly when that sink is on — so there is no state where it is hidden and still costing a pass. The deck previews under it are auditions of their own, so they stay when it goes.

The picture carries no label of its own. The bay head already says Program, and this is a region somebody may be capturing: a capture that is neither the canvas nor a clean crop of it is worse than useless, and a word burnt into the corner is exactly that. The A–D under it keep their letters, which are outside anything you would capture and are the only thing naming a deck.

The picture is the canvas's shape, and it is the console that makes it so. It is as large as the region allows and centred in it, which is the rule a deck preview cell already follows — so the rectangle is a clean crop rather than something to crop, and the bars that used to be rendered inside it every frame are not rendered at all. Drag the program's height and the picture grows with it, because the height is what you are given to answer how big should the picture be.

Widen the window far enough and the four deck previews move beside the picture — A and B down the left, C and D down the right — because a wide bay leaves ground down each side and the picture can have it instead. Whichever arrangement leaves the larger picture is the one you get, so it flips once on the way out and once on the way back, at the same width both times: nothing is remembered, and a window dragged wide and back comes back to exactly what it left. The cells keep the size they had in the row — whatever you last dragged the row's height to, and that carries across the flip rather than being recomputed from the bay — and each keeps its letter where it was: C is at the top of the right-hand side because it was third in the row, and it stays there whether or not C is running. Fold the picture away and they go back under it: there is nothing for them to be beside.

What is beside it is the bay, and a capture pays for that. The leftover is the Program bay's own card, not black. Solo the program view and the window is that region, so a window whose shape is not the canvas's captures panel-coloured bars where black would read as an ordinary letterbox — size the window to the canvas and there are none. That is what a is for at the engine's own window, and this one has not got it yet.

What a deck preview cell shows, and when

A cell is its slot's monitor and shows that slot's own material. Not the mix it would join, and not a still or a thumbnail of it: the deck's texels, moving, through the transfer curve the audience gets. The channel fader is not in it. The fader is the step after the decision — you look at the cell, you decide, and then you raise the fader and the material goes to the picture — so a monitor carrying the fader would show the decision back instead of the material it is made on, and a deck at zero would be a black cell for exactly the moment it is being judged in.

Residency does not gate a cell. A deck that is priming or allocated is the one worth looking at, because it is the candidate and its residency is what you have not decided yet. So a cell is not lit by its deck going on air and does not go dark when it comes off: load something into a slot and it starts running and appears in that slot's cell, at whatever residency, for as long as something is in there. A is on air in the panel above and C is parked, and both cells are drawn.

The letter, the state and the cost are written under the image and never on it. A caption is a row of its own below each cell: the deck's letter, one word for what the cell is showing, and a coloured dot for what the slot costs. It is outside the image because a letter laid over the material is unreadable exactly when the deck is live and the material is bright — which is the one moment the row is being read fastest — and because the material is what the image is for. The letter is welded to the cell. Nothing routes a preview, so A is A whatever is loaded, and a cell with nothing behind it keeps its letter and its place rather than sliding its neighbours along.

A cell that has nothing to show says which nothing it is, and none of the nothings is being off air. The one the instrument can be in today is no slot: the deck has fewer slots than the row has cells, so the fourth has nothing to sample at all — deck D above. Two more are named and neither can happen yet, so nothing draws either word: a slot that exists with nothing loaded into it is empty, and a build that was rejected, over budget or failed, shows black with the sentence saying what went wrong on the slot it went wrong on. What a cell never does is show nothing at all, because an operator cannot tell that apart from a slot with nothing in it and would go looking for the material rather than for the fault. The staging lane says the same thing at more length, and the cell is where you are already looking.

The badge is what the slot costs, read as how many of these you can mix and at what rate. Four slots share one frame, so 16.7 ms at 60 Hz is about 4 ms each, and the bands answer the question an operator actually has rather than handing them a number to divide in a dark room. Green, up to 4 ms: four of these at 60 Hz. Blue, over 4 ms: not four at 60, and two at 60 is fine — two on air and two being prepared is the ordinary way of working, so blue is the ordinary state of good material and not a caution. Yellow, about 8 ms: the boundary for two at 60 Hz, and four at 30 Hz. Red, about 12 ms: one at 60 Hz, two at 30 Hz. Purple, over 16 ms: one slot eats a whole frame and the cell stops drawing. That last one is the point of the scale rather than its last entry — a slot over budget is stopped, not shown harder, and nothing about the frame rate is lowered to accommodate it. A value on a boundary rounds to the worse band.

The estimate exists now, and the dot is what is left. This paragraph said there was no cost estimate for a slot anywhere in this workspace, and a badge drawn hollow or drawn green by default would assert a reading nobody took. That was true when it was written and stopped being true in two steps: a renderer's floor is bounded from what its parameters declare, so an estimate answers for material it used to refuse, and the governor now budgets on that number where it has one and on the measurement where it does not. So the reading a band would assert is taken, by something, every frame — and what is missing is the drawing, the table that turns a number into one of the five bands, and a path from the deck to this caption. The letter and the state are drawn; the badge is the last part of this cell the panel cannot fill, and it is no longer the estimate that is missing.

The cells are not outputs. The picture beside them is one — it carries the master mix, it is listed in Outputs as program view, and once material is on it, it is the broadcast of the final result rather than a preview of anything. A cell is the other kind of thing: nothing routes it, nothing chooses what goes to it, and each is welded to the letter in its caption. That is why every sink can be off and the cells keep running.

A cell is a drop target as well as a monitor. A Set carried out of the Library bay and let go on one names that cell's deck, which is the same load a release on that deck's strip asks for — and these cells are in the centre column beside the library, while the mixer is a window away. The cell under the pointer is ringed while the Set is in hand and nothing else is, and the ring is round the cell rather than over the image, for the reason the letter is under the image and not on it. A cell whose letter names no slot is the exception, and the only one: the row is four cells whatever the deck holds, so on a deck of fewer slots the last cells have no deck to load into — a release there loads nothing and nothing is ringed, which is the refusal the keyboard's own 0–3 meet on the same count. Nothing else about a cell is read for it: what it is showing never decides. How a Set reaches a deck is where that mark is specified.

What a model is refused, and where a class opens

Four indicators, one per class: this bay's head, the Mixer's, the Master's, and the Outputs row, which has no head of its own and takes the pill beside the word that stands in for one. Each opens one class of operations to a model, and each starts shut. The list a model is offered never shortens. Every operation in the vocabulary is connected to it, a shut class included; what shut changes is that the call is answered with a refusal instead of being performed. That is the harder of the two mechanisms to build and it is the one worth building — a tool a model cannot see is not a tool it was refused, it is a tool the first rule says exists and this surface says does not.

Shut, the pill is the ordinary capsule and reads mcp · shut; open, it reads mcp · open and is drawn armed — the same .pill.armed treatment the transport's audio‑in pill carries, for that pill's own reason. A pill that only changed colour would leave which class unanswered, and a pill that only changed its word would make a shut class and an open one look alike at the distance a panel is read from; the word answers the first question and the treatment answers the second, and a class is both questions at once. Every one of the four is drawn shut above because every class starts shut — open is that same pill after the press its tooltip promises, and never a second indicator beside it.

A refusal can say why and an absence cannot. A model that finds no tool learns nothing, plans around a hole it has no name for, and reports back that this instrument cannot do a thing it can do — the same failure as a frame that comes out plausible and wrong: nothing breaks, nothing is logged, and the person who could have fixed it with one press never hears about it. So a refusal names the class, says an operator can open it, and says which bay the pill is in. The person sitting beside the model is the one who can act on that, which is who it is written for as much as the model.

The toggle belongs to the map rather than to the instrument. Every surface reaches the vocabulary through a layer that says which gesture names which operation — the ⊕ MIDI: line every tooltip here ends with is that layer, one control at a time — and whether a surface may reach a class of operations at all is the same layer's second job. This pill is not a control over the mixer or over the program, then: it is the same editor as the icon in a tooltip, entered from the other end, per class instead of per control. It is also why the opening is not an operation of its own. The first rule would make it reachable from all four surfaces, and a permission an actor can grant itself is not a permission.

What a shut class costs a model is a plan it finds out about on the fourth call. The list it read at the start of a session is still true at the end of one, by construction, so nothing announces a class being shut while a model is three calls into something: it learns by being refused, and the refusal is the whole of what it has to work from. Whether anything should be pushed rather than discovered that way is not settled here. Neither is whether one press opens a class or a single operation of it, whether an opening outlives a restart, or whether an open class shuts itself again after a while. Nor is whether a bay carrying no class draws the pill at all — the Library, the Inspector, Staging and the Sequencer have none, and an indicator that is present everywhere reads as a state wherever it is absent.

Outputs

Every place a composited frame goes, as one switchable list. A projector window is a second sink and lives in the repository; Syphon, Spout and NDI are plugin sinks and appear only when a plugin is loaded. Fullscreen is just what an application does. The picture in the Program bay is a sink like any other and is the first row.

All of them may be off, and that is a state worth having. The deck previews are monitors rather than outputs — nothing routes one and each is fixed to its own deck — so with every sink off you can still see the decks and work on them, which is what setting up before doors is. The picture is the one thing in the Program bay that is an output, and it is this row's first. Nothing is refused here, so nothing has to be explained: a control that quietly declines the last of something is a rule an operator can only find by experiment.

Every icon explains itself

Density is only earned if it is legible on demand, so hover says three things: what the control is, what state it is in, and what a click will do. The third is the one usually left out, and leaving it out is what makes a compact panel unlearnable — the reader sees the state and never learns it is a control. The tooltip also carries the control's MIDI assignment, which is how learning one works: point at anything with a tooltip and move a knob. Coverage then takes care of itself — a new operation gets a tooltip, and a tooltip is a thing a map can reach.

One vocabulary, four ways in

Every operation is reachable from the GUI, from the keyboard alone, from a mapped MIDI control, and from MCP. That is structural before it is visual: each operation is named once, and every surface routes into that name. An operation only the mouse can reach breaks it; so does one the map cannot address. The sequencer is a fifth route, and it only works for the same reason.

GUI → command keys → command MIDI map → command MCP → command sequencer → command

It is checkable rather than merely intended: with the vocabulary exhaustive, a test asserts that every command carries a key binding, is addressable by the map, and is what the GUI emits. And it is half-built — L4:1:exposure is already one spelling shared by --param, --publish, the MIDI map and the MCP tools. So this milestone opens with naming, not drawing.

What a surface routes through is a map, and one surface has one. A map is the layer between a gesture and the vocabulary, and it does more than one job: which gesture names which operation, whether that surface may reach a class of operations at all — the mcp pill is that second job, per class instead of per control — and the helpers that carry a musical instant into a record. Only the MIDI surface has that layer as data today. The keyboard's bindings are written into the program, the panel's are the derivations that draw each control, and MCP's are the names of the tools it publishes. So none the map cannot address is a rule about one surface and an intention about three, and it is said here rather than in the seven rules because it is the map's gap and not the vocabulary's.

Where the tracker's own doubt goes

The estimator can already say it thinks the grid is at half the music's tempo — it is x2? on the status line the program prints, shown only when the estimate behind it is worth trusting — and it is the thing that tells a performer to reach for ×2 at all. The panel draws the control and not the prompt, which leaves the operator to notice for themselves the one thing the instrument already knows. It needs a home in the transport, and it needs deciding next to a second fact: a grid at half the music's tempo can only be doubled below 100 BPM, so above that the hint and the refusal would arrive together.

A layout, not a specification — it is what the implementation is checked against. The mechanisms behind the mixer, the inspector, the library and the outputs are built, and so are the Master bay's out and the staging lane's row; the three effects of the master chain, the sequencer, the two acts a candidate row would offer, authority and the panel itself are not. The seven rules are what all of this obeys.
