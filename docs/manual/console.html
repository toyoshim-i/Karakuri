The console is the instrument

Pick a Set per deck, mix them live, and see who is holding every control. Mixing lives between decks; shaping lives inside one. Every divider drags, because a preview's size is a machine's answer rather than a layout's — and the whole panel follows the room, pastel on white by day and lit on black in a dark hall. The grip in a bay's head says whose size you are setting: a bay with one is yours to size, and a bay without one is the height of what is in it. Both sides of a divider move either way; the grip says which side the number belongs to.

Three columns, and they are named because you address them: a left pane and a right pane, which fold away to give room, and the centre, which is what they give it to. A pane folds by dragging its boundary out: pull it past the narrowest the pane goes and the pane closes, keeping its outer edge — a gutter at the window's own edge where the pane used to be. Drag that edge back in and the pane returns at its narrowest. Nothing is drawn there while the pane is open, because until it is closed the pane's own boundary is where it always was. Each holds a stack of bays — Library, Program, Mixer and the rest — and every bay below says what it is standing on. The middle one is not a third pane on purpose: folding it is not a thing anybody wants, and solo is.

The engine already said which one is the fader

An earlier draft drew gain and opacity as two identical sliders, which threw away a distinction the code states outright:

/// The level the material arrives at. Colour only.
pub gain: f32,
/// The fader across the blend, [0, 1]. The only one of the two that
/// touches what a layer covers.
pub opacity: f32,

They behave differently too: opacity at zero silences under every blend mode, and gain at zero does not silence over — a black card still covers. So this is a channel fader and a trim, as a mixer has always had them. Opacity is the tall control your hand lives on; gain is the small trim above it, set once and left.

The mixer is an L5, and so is a master effect

A master effect looked like a new kind of node. The engine already writes the signature down:

// The L5 node, nested: `[Texture] -> Texture`.

A master effect is that signature with one input; the mixer is the same with several. One kind, not two — what made the mixer look special is that its instance is built in, the third time this shape has come up: L3 has a built-in orbit camera and writable camera procedures, and the merge record describes the built-in L5 exactly as camera describes the built-in L3.

So admitting frame effects is giving L5 a writable form, not extending the algebra. Fan-in is already solved by uses plus edge; a per-deck effect and a master effect become the same node at different points; and the deck count stops being a system constant, since how many inputs an L5 folds becomes a property of the procedure rather than of one built-in shader. Feedback is the exception: reading the previous frame is a cycle, and wants a decision of its own.

What each region is standing on

Mixer

A channel strip per filled track, all visible, nothing that scrolls out of reach mid transition. Four tracks because the compositing shader declares four inputs, and a track nothing fills draws nothing at all — so the strip is a paged list whose length is a number, the header says how long the list is, and it says which page you are on. Three strips above, because the deck the mock is drawing has three slots filled.

The residency chip is the one control that can be showing two things at once. What a deck is doing and what it was asked to do are separate facts: the budget may hold a slot below the request and never above it, so a deck asked to prime with no room sits parked — the request standing, reconsidered every pass, primed the moment there is room. Drawn as allocated and nothing else, that is indistinguishable from a deck nobody asked for anything, and the only way to tell would be to ask again. So the chip rolls part of the way toward the residency it was asked for and falls back, about once a second, and never lands: the settled word is where the deck is, the word coming up behind it is where it is going, and that it keeps falling back is how it says it has not arrived. Deck C is drawn that way above.

A fader with a fade scheduled on it says the same three things, and a track can say one of them better. A transition is armed on the beat grid, so with the default quantum it is due up to a bar after the key that asked for it — and a control that changes something invisible is indistinguishable from a control that is broken. So the knob and the fill go on saying where the fader is, a hairline across the track says where it is going, and the fill keeps setting off toward that line and falling back, once a second, never covering the gap. Deck B is drawn with a fade to 0.80 armed on it. What a capsule could not say and a track can is which way: the mark is a position, read exactly as the knob's own position is read, so the distance to it is the size of the move. The number under the fader stays the value the deck is at, because that is the thing you are about to reach for.

Those two are the only things on this page that move, they move at one rate off one phase, and each moves only while something is pending — a console with nothing outstanding is completely still. The mask's front is the gap: a wipe is a fade on a number this strip has no control for and no room to show, so an armed wipe is the one scheduled move the mixer cannot draw, and it is named here rather than left to be discovered.

It is a control as well as a readout, and the press counts from what was asked for rather than from what it shows. A click asks for the next of the three — live, priming, allocated, wrapping — taken from the residency this deck was last asked for. On a settled deck the two are the same value and the chip behaves exactly as it looks. On a parked one they are not, and that is the case worth knowing: the chip shows allocated and was asked for priming, so a press asks for allocated — which is the prime request withdrawn. Counting from the word on the chip instead would ask for live, and a press meant to take a request back would put the deck on air. It is the same choice the keyboard already makes: w on a parked deck withdraws.

The chip refuses nothing. A pending transition is a thing the surface says, never a lock it holds — what may be asked for is the instrument's to decide, so every way in meets the same wall, and go on air is always one of them.

The mixer has no crossfader

The mix is the channel faders. A crossfader over the same two decks could only ever be a readout — the mark says where those two faders have already put the pair and is never a number of its own, and a third control over the same two numbers is a second way to say one thing. What it would have bought is not bought here: a constrained sum under one hand, two opacities moved together because one grip moves both. That is the two-deck cut, and this is four strips where the fader is the work. Crossfade to the next deck is reached by no control here and by no key: a crossfade is scheduled, and a scheduled move is asked for by a route with a long turnaround rather than authored on a real-time surface. MCP is that route today.

Nothing on a strip starts a fade

A hand on the fader is the fade. An opacity moved over a few seconds is what a fade is, and a fader is already the control for moving one — so a capsule asking for the same move would take permanent room on a strip to save a gesture the strip is built around. What it would have bought is the one thing a hand cannot do: land the move on the beat rather than near it. That is worth having and it is not worth a control here. The keyboard is the same surface with the same rule, so Fade a deck out or in is reached by no key either: what a hand does here is move the fader, and the scheduled form is asked for by a route with a long turnaround — MCP today.

A fade arriving is still drawn, and that is a different thing: the fader carries the hairline and the fill reaches for it, because a move somebody else scheduled is a move you have to be able to see. Take the fader and it is cancelled — the operator wins.

Inspector

n panes, each showing whatever you point it at. Parameters group by where they land, which for most of them is one node, addressed the way a Set is addressed everywhere else (L2:0:amount) so an effect layer's controls need no second idea of where things live. Which rows carry a fader is what a Set publishes — the reason a deck of twenty-five declared knobs can be six controls on the desk. A control the interface does not carry keeps its row and loses its number and its fader, because this is the bay where that choice is made and a choice you cannot see is one you cannot unmake.

A control that lands in more than one node is not one of those, and it is not rare. It goes in the group at the top, which is what the rest of this section is mostly about: it is a published control with no address, it moves every node that declares its key, and a Set that declares no interface at all publishes nothing else.

The pane scrolls, and it says how much it is not showing

A deck's node groups come to more than a pane holds — one geometry publishing nineteen knobs is a single group 454 pixels tall, against an Inspector bay that is allowed down to 151.5 — so the pane's body scrolls under its two heads. The showing row and the deck head stay: which deck you are looking at and what its clock is doing are the two things that must never leave the screen, because everything under them is only legible once you know whose they are.

The wheel over the pane is the way in, three parameter rows a notch, and the pointer decides which pane — two panes are two positions and the wheel turns the one it is over. The keyboard's way in is not bound here. A bay's items are walked with the arrows, this bay's items are its panes and a pane's are its node groups, and the operations page names no key for that walk yet; a key invented on this side would be the panel deciding what the page reserved. So there is one route today and it is the pointer's, which is a gap and not a decision.

A pane showing part of itself says so, in the count at the right of its head — 5 of 5 above, which is the Library foot's 5 of 27 read on the groups a pane stacks rather than on the rows a listing holds. It counts what is whole: a group cut by the top or the bottom edge is drawn as far as the pane goes and is not counted, so the number and the picture cannot disagree. That is rule 04 met by the one part of it that is hard — says how much — and it is why the count is groups and not pixels: a bay's items are what an operator counts, and a percentage is a number about a rectangle.

There is no bar down the side, and the count is the mark instead. This console draws no scrollbar anywhere, and the reason is not restraint: a bar you can drag is a control, which owes a row on the operations page and a key and a map line beside it, and a bar you cannot drag is a shape that looks like a control and refuses the hand — which is rule 03 broken by a thing that says nothing about what a click will do because a click does nothing. A group drawn to the pane's edge is the second half of the mark, and it is the half that says which way there is more.

The position is the pane's own, and a resize does not rewrite it. It is the cursor in the library list one bay over: no operation names it, nothing outside the console could be the record of what it is, and it is not part of the arrangement — so saving an arrangement does not carry it and restoring one does not move it. Drag the pane taller and it draws more of what it holds; drag it shorter and it draws less, and what you scrolled to is still there when the pane comes back, exactly and not nearly. A pane that clamped its own position while being drawn would lose an operator's place every time a window got smaller, which is the same slow leak the arrangement refuses one level out.

What this replaces is a pane that drew a group whole or not at all. That rule was honest while there was nowhere to scroll to — a node head with two of its five parameters under it reads as a node with two parameters — but its price was that a short pane drew no knob at all, and a bay whose whole purpose is the knobs cannot answer a small window with an empty pane. With a position to scroll, a part-drawn group is no longer a lie about what the node has: the count says how many are whole, and the rest of the group is one notch away.

A control that names no one node

A published control carries an address or it does not. Addressed, it is one node's and it is drawn in that node's group — L4:0:exposure is a row under L4:0, and the group head is the address. Unaddressed, it is one control over every node that declares its key: exposure published bare is a single knob moving both renderers, with a range narrowed to the part both of them said they still look like themselves over. Two shapes, one interface, and the second has no group below to sit in.

It is also the ordinary case rather than the exotic one. A Set that declares no interface publishes one control per key and nothing else — a bare name is how you reach every declaration of it, so publishing one control per declaration would put two controls called exposure on a desk, which is a shape the instrument refuses outright. So on a Set nobody wrote an interface for, every row on this panel names no one node, and a panel that grouped only by node would draw an empty pane under a headline promising groups.

So they go first, in a group of their own, and its head says what it stands over instead of where it is. ∗ across nodes · 2 nodes is the address a bare name actually has: not a place, but a reach. The rows under it are parameter rows like any other and carry the same mark, so a control the operator can see is a wildcard is one they will not be surprised by later. The head carries no man / sug / auto, for the reason the folded renderers head below carries none: authority is one node's, and this head is not a node.

First rather than last, because on a Set with no interface it is the whole panel and every group below it is empty, and a pane that opens with a column of empty node heads before it reaches a knob is a pane read back to front.

The two arrangements it beats, and what each would have cost. A row in every group it touches draws one control as n rows — and the number in the left-hand column is a MIDI position, so knob 6 would appear twice, anyone counting rows would count more controls than the deck published, and take back beside two of them would be one binding wearing two faces. A fold, closed by default puts the majority case behind a press, which is the one place hiding something quietly costs most.

A wildcard over exactly one node is that node's, and is drawn there unmarked. Nothing is invented by doing so: every node that declares the key is a set the Set itself decides, and where it has one member there is no second group the row could go in. The cost is that the row moves. Give this Set a second geometry that also declares radius and that row leaves L1:0 for the group at the top, having been touched by nobody — which is the honest consequence of one control per key, and is said here rather than discovered on stage.

What no grouping can fix is that a wildcard crosses authority. Authority is one node's; a bare exposure writes both renderers in one motion. So a renderer handed to an agent hands it, through that control, a renderer you kept — rule 06's switch arriving by the back door at the width of a key. What this bay owes is to make the reach visible before the press, and the group head and the row's mark are that. What happens on the write is not this page's to settle: the operation that moves a parameter carries the same optional address the control does, and nothing in the vocabulary yet says what an authority may refuse.

The deck head, and why it is in the inspector

A head is a strip of chips above the thing it heads, and this is the first one that heads a deck: its clock, its size, its seed and its fold, over the node groups that are its shape. It is in the inspector because the inspector is where everything about what a deck is lives, and a deck is running material with a time of its own. The mixer could not take it: the mixer holds only what decides how much of each deck reaches the screen, and a strip is 53 pixels wide with nothing spare in it. It sits under the pane's head rather than in it, because the pane's head says which deck is showing and this says what that deck is doing — and because seven chips and a deck's name do not fit on one row of a 237-pixel pane.

Sync is one chip that cycles, and the cycle skips. Free, tempo, beat. Tempo scales the rate a deck advances at; beat locks its position, which needs the ability to jump, so it is offered only where the material is closed form and can be evaluated at a moment. Material that accumulates can be run forward and not placed, so beat is skipped rather than shown greyed — and the reason is what the chip says. Tempo is skipped in the other direction, on material that already reads the beat, because scaling its clock as well would make it follow the room twice. Deck A above is on tempo for the first reason and deck B is on beat.

The anchor reads two numbers and asks for one thing, and the offset beside it is the scrub's own value. T128 is the tempo a deck was engaged at, which is what its rate is measured against; B128 +0.25 is that with the deck sitting a quarter beat ahead of the room. A free deck shows neither, because free is the absence of a transport rather than a setting, and a column reading free on every deck would be four words of nothing — so a free deck has no anchor to press either, which costs it nothing, since free is the one mode that never reads an anchor.

Pressing the anchor re-anchors: it asks for the mode this deck is already in. Engaging a mode takes the room's tempo as the reference the material's rate is measured against, so engaging the one that is already running is how an operator says call this the reference tempo — the picture does not move, the rate goes back to 1×, and the scrub goes back to zero with it. It needs no operation of its own; it is the deck's own mode, named. What it needs is a control that can name it, and the chip on the left structurally cannot. A cycle starts at the mode after the one you are on, so the one state it can never arrive at is the one you are in — and the single case where it does land there is material that accumulates and reads the beat, where free is the only mode left and free reads no anchor. That is a fault of the affordance and not a gap in what can be asked for: every control that names a mode outright gets re-anchoring the day it exists, a pad naming one and a call naming one alike, and only a cycle is shut out of it.

Scrub moves by an amount, which nothing else on this panel does — a quarter beat a press, into that same offset, signed and unbounded because a scrub is the one control here meant to go backwards. It is live only where the deck is beat-synced, since that is the only mode that reads the offset, and where it is not the arrows keep their shape and say why. Nothing is shown as a price, because nothing re-runs a history: placing accumulating material would mean running it again from where it started, and that is not an operation this instrument has.

Composite is the deck's, and the renderer chips below are what it turns into a choice. On, a deck's renderers fold into one result and one of them is live; off, they are all overdrawn. A deck publishing a single renderer draws the chip anyway and says that it changes nothing either way, because a deck that grows a second one needs the control already where it was.

Pressing it re-aims the slot, and that is worth saying out loud because it looks like a setting and is a rebuild. How a slot lays its renderers up is one field of the description its watcher is pointed at — beside the files, the fold, the capacity, the salts, the camera and the wiring — so a press restates the rest of that description with this one field changed and hands it over. The worker recompiles the slot off the render thread, the result lands at a frame boundary, and it is judged on that Set's own measured frame against the frame budget exactly as a saved file and a library load are. A build that cannot hold the frame stays in the slot and the slot stops updating, which the staging lane and that deck's caption both say. Nothing is installed and nothing allocates on the render thread, and the values your hands have moved cross the rebuild rather than snapping back to what the files declare.

That is the reason this is a chip and not a switch, and the reason the verdict is somewhere else. Compositing gives every renderer its own frame-sized target, so the press is asking for memory and for a fold pass, and this instrument does not let anything reach the screen that nothing measured. What happened to a press is therefore in the staging lane, on the deck it happened to, like every other build — and the deck head chip goes on saying what the deck is doing rather than what was asked for, so the two can be read against each other. Which renderer was live is restated with the layering, so overdrawing a deck and folding it back comes back on the renderer you left it on.

The capacity and the salts sit in that same description, and they are the two chips before the fold. A geometry's element count and the salt that decides which randomness it got are fields of the aim beside the layering, so the mechanism that moves them is the one the fold uses and no writer into a live Set is wanted or would be allowed. What was missing was never the mechanism — it was the control, and what held the control up was that the obvious one is refused. A capacity is a number in a declared range, which everywhere else on this panel is a parameter row's track; but a parameter track dragged is a uniform write a frame and a capacity track dragged would be a rebuild a frame, dozens of them across one gesture, each reallocating every element buffer in the slot. So the capacity steps instead, through the powers of two inside the range this deck's geometries declare, wrapping at each end — the affordance the library's filter field already uses, for the same reason and one bay along.

The chip reads what the slot is running at, and it is lit exactly when somebody asked for that number. Unlit is the geometry's own declared default, which is where every run opens, so the two states answer who chose this rather than repeating the number in a second place. The step is the console's arithmetic and what leaves it is the number arrived at, never a step: two hands stepping one control would otherwise disagree about where it is. The chip is the slot's and not one node's. A Set holding two geometries runs both at this number, which is what --capacity has always meant; a capacity for one geometry of a pair is not something the aim can carry, so it is not something this chip offers, and a deck whose geometries declare no range they share has nothing to step through and keeps its shape without claiming the press.

re-salt re-seeds the slot and touches nothing structural. Every hash builtin is salted, so hash1(seed) moves and seed % 512u does not — the colours, the jitter and the scatter change, and the lattice, the ordering and the count stay where they were. The salt is not invented here. Each press takes the next in this slot's own sequence, derived from the salt it is running, so the same slot re-salted from the same place twice lands on the same two pictures and a deck kept afterwards writes the salts it was running at into the Set file. That is the difference between a re-seed and a shuffle: nothing in this instrument produces a number a later run cannot produce again. There is no way back by pressing — the sequence goes forward — so what recovers a look you stepped past is the Set you kept of it.

A pane too narrow for the two keeps the five that were here before them, and this is the one row on the console that drops part of itself rather than all of it. The reason is a measurement: a pane at the smallest window this arrangement claims is 237.5 pixels wide and the row needs about 296 for all seven, so a row that took all of them or none would draw nothing at the width the console says it works at — trading two controls that were never there for four that were. With two panes open the two appear at a window of about 1360 and are there at every ordinary size above it; a third pane takes them away again. That is a limit named here rather than one an operator finds by dragging, and the honest reading of it is that the deck head is now the row this console has the least room left in.

An input is wired on the node that declares it

A procedure declares the inputs it takes and never the nodes that fill them — uses far : Geometry, and the file says far and stops. That is not a formality: a part that names the parts around it is bound to one Set and stops being a library part, and a corpus of procedures that cannot be recombined is not a corpus. So the node names the input and the Set names the node, and this line is where the Set says it.

It is a capsule with a card under it, the Library bay's deck pulldown one bay along. A press brings down the nodes of the kind the input takes — a Geometry input lists this deck's geometries, a Field input its fields — and the node that declares the input is not in its own list. A pick replaces: an input takes one node, a Set built with two edges on one input is refused where it is built, and there is no way to leave an input empty because a Set with an empty input does not build at all. So this control has no off, and that is the language's shape rather than a control that forgot one.

A pick re-aims the slot, which is the deck head's three chips one row up and the same mechanism: the wiring is one field of what this slot's watcher is pointed at, so the pick restates the rest and the worker recompiles the slot off the render thread. The staging lane carries the verdict. Nothing is refused on this side: the console offers the nodes it can see and a name the Set cannot use is refused where the Set is built, by name and with what the Set does hold — the same wall a model's wire_input meets, in the same sentence.

The line is drawn only where there is an input to draw, which on most decks is nowhere: a geometry and a renderer declare no inputs, so most node groups have no line here at all. What decides it is the procedure, not the panel.

The number is the mark, and publishing is a choice of attention

The figure at the left of a parameter row is where that control sits in this deck's published interface, which is what a MIDI knob counts. It is also the control that decides whether the row is on the interface at all: press it and the control comes off, press it again and it goes back on at the end of the list. A row that is off keeps its place in its group and loses its number, its fader and its figure.

What that changes is what is shown, never what can be reached. A control nobody published is still written by --param, by a param record in a Set file and by a model naming its address. If publishing gated reach, a Set's author could lock an operator out of their own machine, and this instrument's position everywhere else is the opposite: show every measurement, hide nothing, needing a hand is craft rather than defect.

The numbers below it move, and that is worth knowing before you press. A knob is learned against a position, so taking a control out from under one renumbers everything after it and the knobs follow the numbers rather than the names. That is also why a press restates the whole list rather than saying drop this one: adding or removing one entry at a time is a statement about an entry, and what this is about is the list.

An interface that publishes nothing publishes everything, which is where every deck opens: nobody has narrowed anything, so every declared control has a number and a fader and no row is faint. The first mark you press is what turns the list into an interface. Deck A above has been narrowed — sphere_shell's two knobs are off it — and deck B beside it has not.

A press re-aims the slot, like the wiring above it and the deck head's three chips: the interface is one field of what the slot's watcher is pointed at, so a narrowing survives every later rebuild rather than being wiped by the next save of any file in the deck. What it costs is a recompile for a choice about a display, and what it buys is that the choice is still there afterwards. A replay does not reproduce it and neither does a keep: no record in this format says what a Set publishes, so a narrowing lives in the run that made it. That is a gap this page names rather than one you find later.

A node's source is not read or written here, and that is settled

There is no source area in this bay and there is not going to be one. Read one node's source and check and write one node's source both carried a panel route on the operations page that pointed at this bay; on 2026-09-09 they stopped, and the two rows now say the panel is not a way in at all. A model has both halves over MCP and is where the work is done.

The reason is the console's own bound and not a shortage of room. This console takes letters in two places — an arrangement's name, and a Set's id typed into a pane head above — and each of them is bounded to one path component. A procedure is unbounded text, so an editor here is a third flow rather than the second one again, and it is exactly the bound those two were held to that it would break. Sequencer

One row per lane. The row keeps its width and the cells divide it, so eight steps are landscape cells and sixteen are portrait ones. Each lane sits under the mixer strip it drives.

A lane is a fifth route into the vocabulary, and not a binding. It emits operations on the beat, the way the pointer, the keys, a map and a model do — which is the same sentence One vocabulary, four ways in below already made about the sequencer, and this note used to contradict it. A binding cannot be what a lane is, on two counts. Its target is a parameter inside a Set, and three of the four lanes drawn here are deck faders, which are no Set's. And a binding does not yield to a hand: it is a projection recomputed every frame, so a lane built that way would rewrite the fader you were holding sixty times a second — a display that moves when you push it rather than a control. Every hand-driven setter on a deck cancels what was moving it; nothing in the binding path has anywhere to put that cancel. As a route it needs neither: a lane and a hand meet at the same call every other pair of surfaces already meets at.

The step grid is the beat clock subdivided and not a fourth clock — a step is a pure function of the beat, so correcting the tempo changes the rate from now on without moving a beat that has already happened. Two things are worth knowing rather than discovering: a step onset is seen at the next frame, which is right for a sequencer that writes values and would not be for one that fires events; and an eighth at 128 BPM is faster than the band the beat clock's rule was written for — a sixteenth, which is the finer of the two modes the head's pill chooses between, is 117 ms and twice as far outside it.

A cell is a bit and the levels are the lane's. A lane carries an on level and an off level, so an on step writes the first and an off step writes the second: a fader lane with one and zero is a gate, and the same lane with 0.8 and 0.5 is a pulse. An off step that wrote nothing would leave the fader wherever the last on step put it, which is a set of impulses rather than the gate these rows are drawn as. A lane's take-back is the mute rather than the hand. A hand's write lands at once and the lane writes again at the next step, so the way to keep what a hand did is the label beside the row — which is where the second rule's take back sits for a lane. And a lane does not get to cancel a scheduled move. Every hand-driven setter on a deck stops whatever was moving that control and a lane reaches the same setter, so a lane on a fader would otherwise kill a fade onto that deck within one step of it being scheduled, silently. The move is refused instead, and the refusal names the lane that is holding the control, because the next attempt is one press on that lane's label away.

A lane arrives from the foot, muted, with every slot off. That is the one thing about + lane worth knowing before you press it, and it follows from the paragraph above: an off step writes the off level, so a fader lane that arrived driving would hold its deck at zero from the press. The lane is there, its row is drawn, and the label is the press that starts it — the same mute that is its take-back, used the other way round. What the card offers is one channel fader per strip the mixer draws and the published controls of the deck the library's load pulldown names; a deck no inspector pane is showing publishes nothing this console has been handed, so it offers its fader alone.

One thing here is a gap rather than a decision, and it is written down so that it cannot be read as either. A channel fader does not say who is holding it. A bound parameter row shows its source instead of a number — seq 1 is drawn on one in the inspector — and a mixer strip has nowhere to draw the same fact, so three lanes in four would drive a control that says nothing about what is driving it, which is the second rule against the three rows this bay draws first. Either the strip gains a source readout or this sentence is why it does not.

A scheduled move over a fader a lane is driving is refused. A fade, a crossfade or a wipe onto a deck whose channel fader an unmuted lane of the armed pattern drives schedules nothing and writes no record, and the refusal names the lane. Muting that lane — one press on its label — is how the move is asked for again. A muted lane drives nothing and holds nothing, and neither does a lane in a bank that is not armed.

A lane is taken out by the − at the end of its row, and its steps go with it. The lanes below it move up, so a lane's number names whichever lane is at that position now — the master chain addresses a slot the same way. It is not the mute at the other end of the row: a mute keeps the pattern and stops the writes, and this leaves nothing to unmute onto. The foot is where a lane arrives and the row is where one goes.

What tells a procedure row from a Set row is not the badge count either: it is the star. A star is a control over a Set this store holds and is refused on anything else with the id back, so a procedure row has the column and nothing in it.

Two scopes list a procedure and two do not. all gains your own, presets gains the shipped ones. my sets does not, because it is the starred subset of the Sets and there is nothing to star here. folder does not, and the note above is why: a folder row is a take, and there is no take-in for a bare .kir — taking a Set in checks every source against the address its slot record names, and a loose file names nothing. So a directory of .kir files is a directory of parts is still true of somebody else's directory; what has changed is that this program now keeps two directories of parts of its own, with names in them.

Loading one layer makes a Set with no name

A press on a procedure row takes what the deck is playing, writes that one layer over it, and leaves everything else where it is. The layering, the fold, the element counts, the salts, the camera and the wiring are all restated, so nothing comes back as a default; the slot is re-pointed and the worker rebuilds it off the render thread, which is the path a Set load and an edit already take. It lands at a frame boundary, it is judged there on what one frame of it costs, and the Staging lane says which of the three happened. Nothing is installed, and nothing is ever written where the presets are.

It lands on the first node of that kind, and that is a limit written down rather than a rule. L4:0 is the renderer a kind L4 row replaces, so the second renderer of a three-renderer Set is not reachable from this list: a library row cannot say an index, and a payload only one surface could ever fill would be a field for a control nobody has drawn. The day a node head grows a replace this node control is the day it gains one. Where the deck has no node of that kind the procedure is added as node 0 of it — which is the case this is for, since a Set declaring no camera holds the built-in at L3:0 and a kind L3 row takes that place. A node whose declared input nothing binds is refused where the Set is built, naming what the Set does hold: the wall that was already there.

What the deck plays afterwards is a derived Set with no name. The strip reads base + kir, so what is on air says what it is made of rather than claiming to be a Set the library holds, and keep is what gives it a name. Nothing is saved on the press, and that is a decision rather than an omission: a control meant to be pressed repeatedly while you are looking for a picture would leave a row in the library per press, which is the thing my sets exists to keep out of your own shelf, and a disk write hung off a frame boundary is the one cost this instrument refuses.

The edit history goes on filing under the Set the slot started from, which is what keeps the swap cheap to make. A Set load moves what versions are filed under; this one does not. So the history chip keeps listing that deck's versions, and the snapshot every compile takes stays alive over material that has no name of its own. Filing them under nothing would be the literal reading of no name and would empty the walk on the deck you are most actively editing — a version of nothing matches no Set, which is the state a typed pair is already in.

A Set row is unchanged and loads every layer, and a layer it has not got goes empty. Empty means the slot holds no node of that kind, which is the ordinary state of L2 and Field — the pair a bare run opens on is a geometry and a renderer and nothing else. L3 is never empty, because L3:0 addresses something in every Set: a Set declaring no camera holds the built-in orbit, so empty there means the built-in comes back. L1 and L4 cannot be empty at all — a Set with no geometry or no renderer is not a Set.

The library scrolls, and the foot says how much it is not showing

A store holds more Sets than a bay holds rows — twenty-seven against five in the mock, and the number a real library reaches is not bounded by anything — so the list scrolls. The scope chips, the path row, the filter field and the kind chips stay where they are, and so does the foot: they say which library is being read and how much of it you are looking at, and both are only legible while they are on screen.

The wheel over the bay is the way in, three rows a notch, and it is the same notch the inspector's panes take because it is the same wheel and the same row height. It is aimed at the whole bay rather than at the list, so a hand resting over the scope chips turns the listing under them. The keyboard's way in is not bound here. The arrows already walk this bay's items and they walk them inside what is drawn — the cursor cannot leave the rows on screen, because a cursor on a row nobody can see sits under a pill saying a press will load it. Nothing the keyboard could reach before is out of reach now: the rows past the end of the list were unreachable by any means, and they are one notch away. A key that scrolls is owed with the rest of that walk, and inventing one here would be the panel deciding what the operations page reserved.

The count in the foot is what says how much is out of sight — 5 of 27, which is the number this bay has always drawn and which now has a second job. It counts what is whole: a row cut by the top or the bottom edge is drawn as far as the list goes and is not counted, so the number and the picture cannot disagree and 27 of 27 means nothing is out of sight. That is rule 04 met by the one part of it that is hard — says how much — and it is why the count is rows and not pixels: what an operator counts is items.

The reading scrolls with the rows, because it is between two of them. A params block is a mode of the list rather than a card over it, so the rows it pushes down are pushed and not lost, and the block itself is part of what there is to scroll through. Before there was a position, a reading opened near the bottom of a short bay pushed rows off the end with nowhere to reach them; now the block and the rows under it move together and the foot says how many are left.

There is no bar down the side. The reason is the inspector's and is not repeated here except to say that it is the same reason: a bar you can drag is a control, which owes a row on the operations page and a key and a map line beside it, and a bar you cannot drag is a shape that looks like a control and refuses the hand. This console draws no scrollbar anywhere and the library does not add the first one.

The position is this bay's own, and a resize does not rewrite it. It is the cursor above it and the pane positions one bay over: no operation names it, nothing outside the console could be the record of what it is, and it is not part of the arrangement, so saving one does not carry it and restoring one does not move it. Drag the divider up and the bay draws fewer rows of the same position; drag it back and what you scrolled to is still there, exactly and not nearly. One position and not one per scope: only one scope is ever being read, and the press that changes the listing is exactly the moment an operator wants the top.

Walking a Set's edit history

The fifth chip is history, and its rows are versions rather than Sets. Every write that compiles is kept under <store>/history/ — gated on compiling and not on landing, so the version that was rolled back for costing too much is in there and is often the one you want — and this is where they are read. It is a scope on this bay because the three things a walk needs are the three things this bay already does: it lists, it reads, and landing on a row is a load. It is not undo, and the word is avoided here for the same reason the operations page avoids it: what versions has this had is a list, and landing on one is a load.

It is narrowed to a Set, and the Set is the one the load pulldown's deck is running. Not the deck: two decks playing one Set have one history between them, and the deck is only how this bay arrives at the id. So the letter in the foot points the listing as well as the load, and it is the one control that moves these rows — the scope chips, the filter field, the kind chips and the cursor do not. A deck playing a pair somebody typed lists nothing, and that is an answer rather than an absence: a version written where there was no Set is filed under none, and a narrowing to a Set matches none of those rather than all of them. The panel says which of the two kinds of empty it is, in the words it says every other empty scope in.

A row is one line, in the name column, and the time is inside it. The mock draws a .dim time beside each Set and the panel draws none, for want of a spelling; a history row does not get one either, because a row of this scope and a row of all are rows of one list and a column drawn for one of them would make them two kinds of thing. So the row is the name the store filed the version under, less the Set id every row here shares: when, which slot, which layer and index, and what the procedure called itself — which is what a person scanning a night's work is scanning for. Most recent first, which is the listing's own order and not this bay's, and the foot's n of m counts the rows drawn against the rows handed over exactly as it does for a library.

Landing on a row is a load, and it installs nothing. The snapshot's bytes are written over that node's working copy under <store>/scratch/ for the deck the pulldown names, and the watcher already looking at that file builds it off the render thread, swaps it at a frame boundary and rolls it back on its own if it cannot hold the budget — the path an edit already takes, and the reason a load was never allowed to hand a built Set to a slot. The version it replaces is kept by the same act, because the rebuild compiles it and compiling is the gate, so a landing you did not mean is itself a row of this listing.

Four refusals, each naming what is in the way and where the deck is still pointed. A deck playing no Set has nothing to put back. A version that is not one of that Set's is not landed on a deck that is playing something else. A node that deck does not hold — the Set it is running has no L3, or fewer of them — is named as what the slot does hold. And a file that is gone is named: rm -rf history/2026/07 is the whole of the retention policy, so a row whose file an operator deleted by hand is an ordinary state of a working store and not a fault.

The chip and the row are two rows of the operations page, and the chip is not the one its neighbours are. A press on the other four asks choose which scope the library shows, whose payload cannot say which was chosen; a press on this one asks walk the edit history, because that is what was asked for and a scope operation emitted here would say a library was chosen for a press that asked for a history. Landing asks put a node's previous version back, which is under Procedures and carries the version an operator picked. There is no third operation, and the cursor moving through these rows asks for nothing, exactly as it asks for nothing in a library.

Program, sized by height

A 16:9 view filling a wide centre column would be 763 pixels tall and eat the inspector whole. So you drag its height and it letterboxes into the width it has — which is what the engine already does. Height given back is rows in the library and the inspector, which are the two bays that take it.

The bay is two regions and they fold apart. The picture is a sink, listed in Outputs as program view, and it is on screen exactly when that sink is on — so there is no state where it is hidden and still costing a pass. The deck previews under it are auditions of their own, so they stay when it goes.

The picture carries no label of its own. The bay head already says Program, and this is a region somebody may be capturing: a capture that is neither the canvas nor a clean crop of it is worse than useless, and a word burnt into the corner is exactly that. The A–D under it keep their letters, which are outside anything you would capture and are the only thing naming a deck.

The picture is the canvas's shape, and it is the console that makes it so. It is as large as the region allows and centred in it, which is the rule a deck preview cell already follows — so the rectangle is a clean crop rather than something to crop, and the bars that used to be rendered inside it every frame are not rendered at all. Fold the picture away and they go back under it: there is nothing for them to be beside.

What is beside it is the bay, and a capture pays for that. The leftover is the Program bay's own card, not black. That is what a is for at the engine's own window, and this one does not get it: a size is a pair of viewport pixels and a key press cannot mean one, which is the same sentence that leaves Move a boundary with no key. What a deck preview cell shows, and when

A cell is its slot's monitor and shows that slot's own material. Not the mix it would join, and not a still or a thumbnail of it: the deck's texels, moving, through the transfer curve the audience gets. The channel fader is not in it. The fader is the step after the decision — you look at the cell, you decide, and then you raise the fader and the material goes to the picture — so a monitor carrying the fader would show the decision back instead of the material it is made on, and a deck at zero would be a black cell for exactly the moment it is being judged in.

Residency does not gate a cell. A deck that is priming or allocated is the one worth looking at, because it is the candidate and its residency is what you have not decided yet. So a cell is not lit by its deck going on air and does not go dark when it comes off: load something into a slot and it starts running and appears in that slot's cell, at whatever residency, for as long as something is in there. A is on air in the panel above and C is parked, and both cells are drawn.

The letter, the state and the cost are written under the image and never on it. A caption is a row of its own below each cell: the deck's letter, one word for what the cell is showing, and a coloured dot for what the slot costs. It is outside the image because a letter laid over the material is unreadable exactly when the deck is live and the material is bright — which is the one moment the row is being read fastest — and because the material is what the image is for. The letter is welded to the cell. Nothing routes a preview, so A is A whatever is loaded, and a cell with nothing behind it keeps its letter and its place rather than sliding its neighbours along.

A cell that has nothing to show says which nothing it is, and none of the nothings is being off air. The one the instrument can be in today is no slot: the deck has fewer slots than the row has cells, so the fourth has nothing to sample at all — deck D above. A cell with a slot behind it says one further thing, and it is not a nothing: overloaded is a slot whose version costs more than one frame may, so the slot stopped updating and the image is the last frame it drew. It is marked because it is a still and stills are what a preview must never pass off as a running picture. One more state is named and cannot happen yet, so nothing draws the word: a slot that exists with nothing loaded into it is empty. A build that was rejected or failed changes no cell at all — nothing was installed, and the slot goes on drawing what it was already playing. What a cell never does is show nothing at all, because an operator cannot tell that apart from a slot with nothing in it and would go looking for the material rather than for the fault. The staging lane says the same thing at more length, and the cell is where you are already looking.

The badge is what the slot costs, read as how many of these you can mix and at what rate. Four slots share one frame, so 16.7 ms at 60 Hz is about 4 ms each, and the bands answer the question an operator actually has rather than handing them a number to divide in a dark room. Green, up to 4 ms: four of these at 60 Hz. Blue, over 4 ms: not four at 60, and two at 60 is fine — two on air and two being prepared is the ordinary way of working, so blue is the ordinary state of good material and not a caution. Yellow, about 8 ms: the boundary for two at 60 Hz, and four at 30 Hz. Red, about 12 ms: one at 60 Hz, two at 30 Hz. Purple, over 16 ms: one slot eats a whole frame and the slot stops. That last one is the point of the scale rather than its last entry — a slot over budget is stopped, not shown harder, and nothing about the frame rate is lowered to accommodate it. Stopped is a state you can see and leave: the slot keeps the frame it last drew, the deck goes on mixing it, the caption reads overloaded, and it stays that way until you fade that deck out, put an earlier version back, or save something that fits. A value on a boundary rounds to the worse band.

The estimate exists now, and the dot is what is left. This paragraph said there was no cost estimate for a slot anywhere in this workspace, and a badge drawn hollow or drawn green by default would assert a reading nobody took. That was true when it was written and stopped being true in two steps: a renderer's floor is bounded from what its parameters declare, so an estimate answers for material it used to refuse, and the governor now budgets on that number where it has one and on the measurement where it does not. So the reading a band would assert is taken, by something, every frame — and what is missing is the drawing, the table that turns a number into one of the five bands, and a path from the deck to this caption. The letter and the state are drawn; the badge is the last part of this cell the panel cannot fill, and it is no longer the estimate that is missing.

The cells are not outputs. The picture beside them is one — it carries the master mix, it is listed in Outputs as program view, and once material is on it, it is the broadcast of the final result rather than a preview of anything. A cell is the other kind of thing: nothing routes it, nothing chooses what goes to it, and each is welded to the letter in its caption. That is why every sink can be off and the cells keep running.

A cell is a drop target as well as a monitor. A Set carried out of the Library bay and let go on one names that cell's deck, which is the same load a release on that deck's strip asks for — and these cells are in the centre column beside the library, while the mixer is a window away. The cell under the pointer is ringed while the Set is in hand and nothing else is, and the ring is round the cell rather than over the image, for the reason the letter is under the image and not on it. A cell whose letter names no slot is the exception, and the only one: the row is four cells whatever the deck holds, so on a deck of fewer slots the last cells have no deck to load into — a release there loads nothing and nothing is ringed, which is the refusal a digit in the mixer meets on the same count. Nothing else about a cell is read for it: what it is showing never decides. How a Set reaches a deck is where that mark is specified.

What a model is refused, and where a class opens

Four indicators, one per class: this bay's head, the Mixer's, the Master's, and the Outputs row, which has no head of its own and takes the pill beside the word that stands in for one. Each opens one class of operations to a model, and each starts shut. The list a model is offered never shortens. Every operation in the vocabulary is connected to it, a shut class included; what shut changes is that the call is answered with a refusal instead of being performed. That is the harder of the two mechanisms to build and it is the one worth building — a tool a model cannot see is not a tool it was refused, it is a tool the first rule says exists and this surface says does not.

What a model calls is one tool, operate, and it names the operation by the same heading the operations page gives it. There is not a tool per class or a tool per row: the vocabulary is the interface, so forty tools would have been forty second spellings of names that already exist. The seven tools beside it stay as they are, because each of them does something the vocabulary alone does not name — reading a file, writing one, reading the store, reporting what a swap did. Every other operation this instrument can perform is asked for by name, audited against these four pills, and then performed on the frame a hand on the panel would have performed it on — the same function, the same thread, the same instant. That is what makes a model a fourth pair of hands rather than a second way to do anything, and it is why a shut class is refused rather than being quietly done somewhere else.

Shut, the pill is the ordinary capsule and reads mcp · shut; open, it reads mcp · open and is drawn armed — the same .pill.armed treatment the transport's audio‑in pill carries, for that pill's own reason. A pill that only changed colour would leave which class unanswered, and a pill that only changed its word would make a shut class and an open one look alike at the distance a panel is read from; the word answers the first question and the treatment answers the second, and a class is both questions at once. Every one of the four is drawn shut above because every class starts shut — open is that same pill after the press its tooltip promises, and never a second indicator beside it.

A refusal can say why and an absence cannot. A model that finds no tool learns nothing, plans around a hole it has no name for, and reports back that this instrument cannot do a thing it can do — the same failure as a frame that comes out plausible and wrong: nothing breaks, nothing is logged, and the person who could have fixed it with one press never hears about it. So a refusal names the class, says an operator can open it, and says which bay the pill is in. The person sitting beside the model is the one who can act on that, which is who it is written for as much as the model.

The toggle belongs to the map rather than to the instrument. Every surface reaches the vocabulary through a layer that says which gesture names which operation — the ⊕ MIDI: line every tooltip here ends with is that layer, one control at a time — and whether a surface may reach a class of operations at all is the same layer's second job. This pill is not a control over the mixer or over the program, then: it is the same editor as the icon in a tooltip, entered from the other end, per class instead of per control. It is also why the opening is not an operation of its own. The first rule would make it reachable from all four surfaces, and a permission an actor can grant itself is not a permission.

What a shut class costs a model is a plan it finds out about on the fourth call. The list it read at the start of a session is still true at the end of one, by construction, so nothing announces a class being shut while a model is three calls into something: it learns by being refused, and the refusal is the whole of what it has to work from. Whether anything should be pushed rather than discovered that way is not settled here. Neither is whether one press opens a class or a single operation of it, whether an opening outlives a restart, or whether an open class shuts itself again after a while. Nor is whether a bay carrying no class draws the pill at all — the Library, the Inspector, Staging and the Sequencer have none, and an indicator that is present everywhere reads as a state wherever it is absent.

Outputs

Every place a composited frame goes, as one switchable list. A projector window is a second sink and lives in the repository; Syphon, Spout and NDI are plugin sinks and appear only when a plugin is loaded. Fullscreen is just what an application does. The picture in the Program bay is a sink like any other and is the first row.

An output is a destination, a size, and whether it is on. The name is a word from a closed list — the program view, a projector window, a plugin sink by its place in the manifest that loaded it — and never the label on the chip, because a projector’s label carries the display it is on and that changes when the cable does. The size is the output’s own: the window manager’s for a window, and the rectangle the Program bay gives the picture for the program view. One frame is composited at the largest enabled output’s size and scaled into every one, so nothing is ever upscaled and nobody with several outputs pays for several renders — and turning a larger sink on raises what every frame costs, which is your own act and is what the Program bay’s size pill reads.

All of them may be off, and that is a state worth having. The deck previews are monitors rather than outputs — nothing routes one and each is fixed to its own deck — so with every sink off you can still see the decks and work on them, which is what setting up before doors is. The picture is the one thing in the Program bay that is an output, and it is this row's first. Nothing is refused here, so nothing has to be explained: a control that quietly declines the last of something is a rule an operator can only find by experiment.

Every icon explains itself

Density is only earned if it is legible on demand, so hover says three things: what the control is, what state it is in, and what a click will do. The third is the one usually left out, and leaving it out is what makes a compact panel unlearnable — the reader sees the state and never learns it is a control. The tooltip also carries the control's MIDI assignment, which is how learning one works: point at anything with a tooltip and move a knob. Coverage then takes care of itself — a new operation gets a tooltip, and a tooltip is a thing a map can reach.

One vocabulary, four ways in

Every operation is reachable from the GUI, from the keyboard alone, from a mapped MIDI control, and from MCP. That is structural before it is visual: each operation is named once, and every surface routes into that name. An operation only the mouse can reach breaks it; so does one the map cannot address. The sequencer is a fifth route, and it only works for the same reason.

GUI → command keys → command MIDI map → command MCP → command sequencer → command

It is checkable rather than merely intended: with the vocabulary exhaustive, a test asserts that every command carries a key binding, is addressable by the map, and is what the GUI emits. And it is half-built — L4:1:exposure is already one spelling shared by --param, --publish, the MIDI map and the MCP tools. So this milestone opens with naming, not drawing.

What a surface routes through is a map, and one surface has one. A map is the layer between a gesture and the vocabulary, and it does more than one job: which gesture names which operation, whether that surface may reach a class of operations at all — the mcp pill is that second job, per class instead of per control — and the helpers that carry a musical instant into a record. Only the MIDI surface has that layer as data today. The keyboard's bindings are written into the program, the panel's are the derivations that draw each control, and MCP's are the names of the tools it publishes. So none the map cannot address is a rule about one surface and an intention about three, and it is said here rather than in the seven rules because it is the map's gap and not the vocabulary's.

Where the tracker's own doubt goes

The estimator can already say it thinks the grid is at half the music's tempo — it is x2? on the status line the program prints, shown only when the estimate behind it is worth trusting — and it is the thing that tells a performer to reach for ×2 at all.
