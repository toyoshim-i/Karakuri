Karakuri · the manual

The console is the instrument

Pick a Set per deck, mix them live, and see who is holding every control. Mixing lives between decks; shaping lives inside one. Every divider drags, because a preview's size is a machine's answer rather than a layout's — and the whole panel follows the room, pastel on white by day and lit on black in a dark hall. The grip in a bay's head says whose size you are setting: a bay with one is yours to size, and a bay without one is the height of what is in it. Both sides of a divider move either way; the grip says which side the number belongs to.

Three columns, and they are named because you address them: a left pane and a right pane, which fold away to give room, and the centre, which is what they give it to. Each holds a stack of bays — Library, Program, Mixer and the rest — and every bay below says what it is standing on. The middle one is not a third pane on purpose: folding it is not a thing anybody wants, and solo is.

The engine already said which one is the fader

An earlier draft drew gain and opacity as two identical sliders, which threw away a distinction the code states outright:

/// The level the material arrives at. Colour only.
pub gain: f32,
/// The fader across the blend, [0, 1]. The only one of the two that
/// touches what a layer covers.
pub opacity: f32,

They behave differently too: opacity at zero silences under every blend mode, and gain at zero does not silence over — a black card still covers. So this is a channel fader and a trim, as a mixer has always had them. Opacity is the tall control your hand lives on; gain is the small trim above it, set once and left.

The mixer is an L5, and so is a master effect

A master effect looked like a new kind of node. The engine already writes the signature down:

// The L5 node, nested: `[Texture] -> Texture`.

A master effect is that signature with one input; the mixer is the same with several. One kind, not two — what made the mixer look special is that its instance is built in, the third time this shape has come up: L3 has a built-in orbit camera and writable camera procedures, and the merge record describes the built-in L5 exactly as camera describes the built-in L3.

So admitting frame effects is giving L5 a writable form, not extending the algebra. Fan-in is already solved by uses plus edge; a per-deck effect and a master effect become the same node at different points; and the deck count stops being a system constant, since how many inputs an L5 folds becomes a property of the procedure rather than of one built-in shader. Feedback is the exception: reading the previous frame is a cycle, and wants a decision of its own.

What each region is standing on

Mixer

Four channel strips, all visible, nothing that scrolls out of reach mid transition. Four today because the compositing shader declares four inputs — the strip is a paged list whose length is a number, and the header says which page you are on.

The residency chip is the one control that can be showing two things at once. What a deck is doing and what it was asked to do are separate facts: the budget may hold a slot below the request and never above it, so a deck asked to prime with no room sits parked — the request standing, reconsidered every pass, primed the moment there is room. Drawn as allocated and nothing else, that is indistinguishable from a deck nobody asked for anything, and the only way to tell would be to ask again. So the chip rolls part of the way toward the residency it was asked for and falls back, about once a second, and never lands: the settled word is where the deck is, the word coming up behind it is where it is going, and that it keeps falling back is how it says it has not arrived. Deck C is drawn that way above.

A fader with a fade scheduled on it says the same three things, and a track can say one of them better. A transition is armed on the beat grid, so with the default quantum it is due up to a bar after the key that asked for it — and a control that changes something invisible is indistinguishable from a control that is broken. So the knob and the fill go on saying where the fader is, a hairline across the track says where it is going, and the fill keeps setting off toward that line and falling back, once a second, never covering the gap. Deck B is drawn with a fade to 0.80 armed on it. What a capsule could not say and a track can is which way: the mark is a position, read exactly as the knob's own position is read, so the distance to it is the size of the move. The number under the fader stays the value the deck is at, because that is the thing you are about to reach for.

Those two are the only things on this page that move, they move at one rate off one phase, and each moves only while something is pending — a console with nothing outstanding is completely still. The mask's front is the gap: a wipe is a fade on a number this strip has no control for and no room to show, so an armed wipe is the one scheduled move the mixer cannot draw, and it is named here rather than left to be discovered.

It is a control as well as a readout, and the press counts from what was asked for rather than from what it shows. A click asks for the next of the three — live, priming, allocated, wrapping — taken from the residency this deck was last asked for. On a settled deck the two are the same value and the chip behaves exactly as it looks. On a parked one they are not, and that is the case worth knowing: the chip shows allocated and was asked for priming, so a press asks for allocated — which is the prime request withdrawn. Counting from the word on the chip instead would ask for live, and a press meant to take a request back would put the deck on air. It is the same choice the keyboard already makes: w on a parked deck withdraws.

The chip refuses nothing. A pending transition is a thing the surface says, never a lock it holds — what may be asked for is the instrument's to decide, so every way in meets the same wall, and go on air is always one of them.

The mixer has no crossfader

The mix is the channel faders. A crossfader over the same two decks could only ever be a readout — the mark says where those two faders have already put the pair and is never a number of its own, and a third control over the same two numbers is a second way to say one thing. What it would have bought is not bought here: a constrained sum under one hand, two opacities moved together because one grip moves both. That is the two-deck cut, and this is four strips where the fader is the work. Crossfade to the next deck keeps its x: the request is a throw, and a throw is asked for at the keyboard.

Inspector

n panes, each showing whatever you point it at. Parameters group by where they land, which for most of them is one node, addressed the way a Set is addressed everywhere else (L2:0:amount) so an effect layer's controls need no second idea of where things live. Only what a Set publishes — the reason this is six rows rather than twenty-five.

A control that lands in more than one node is not one of those, and it is not rare. It goes in the group at the top, which is what the rest of this section is mostly about: it is a published control with no address, it moves every node that declares its key, and a Set that declares no interface at all publishes nothing else.

A control that names no one node

A published control carries an address or it does not. Addressed, it is one node's and it is drawn in that node's group — L4:0:exposure is a row under L4:0, and the group head is the address. Unaddressed, it is one control over every node that declares its key: exposure published bare is a single knob moving both renderers, with a range narrowed to the part both of them said they still look like themselves over. Two shapes, one interface, and the second has no group below to sit in.

It is also the ordinary case rather than the exotic one. A Set that declares no interface publishes one control per key and nothing else — a bare name is how you reach every declaration of it, so publishing one control per declaration would put two controls called exposure on a desk, which is a shape the instrument refuses outright. So on a Set nobody wrote an interface for, every row on this panel names no one node, and a panel that grouped only by node would draw an empty pane under a headline promising groups.

So they go first, in a group of their own, and its head says what it stands over instead of where it is. ∗ across nodes · 2 nodes is the address a bare name actually has: not a place, but a reach. The rows under it are parameter rows like any other and carry the same mark, so a control the operator can see is a wildcard is one they will not be surprised by later. The head carries no man / sug / auto, for the reason the folded renderers head below carries none: authority is one node's, and this head is not a node.

First rather than last, because on a Set with no interface it is the whole panel and every group below it is empty, and a pane that opens with a column of empty node heads before it reaches a knob is a pane read back to front.

The two arrangements it beats, and what each would have cost. A row in every group it touches draws one control as n rows — and the number in the left-hand column is a MIDI position, so knob 6 would appear twice, anyone counting rows would count more controls than the deck published, and take back beside two of them would be one binding wearing two faces. A fold, closed by default puts the majority case behind a press, which is the one place hiding something quietly costs most.

A wildcard over exactly one node is that node's, and is drawn there unmarked. Nothing is invented by doing so: every node that declares the key is a set the Set itself decides, and where it has one member there is no second group the row could go in. The cost is that the row moves. Give this Set a second geometry that also declares radius and that row leaves L1:0 for the group at the top, having been touched by nobody — which is the honest consequence of one control per key, and is said here rather than discovered on stage.

What no grouping can fix is that a wildcard crosses authority. Authority is one node's; a bare exposure writes both renderers in one motion. So a renderer handed to an agent hands it, through that control, a renderer you kept — rule 06's switch arriving by the back door at the width of a key. What this bay owes is to make the reach visible before the press, and the group head and the row's mark are that. What happens on the write is not this page's to settle: the operation that moves a parameter carries the same optional address the control does, and nothing in the vocabulary yet says what an authority may refuse.

The deck head, and why it is in the inspector

A head is a strip of chips above the thing it heads, and this is the first one that heads a deck: its clock and its fold, over the node groups that are its shape. It is in the inspector because the inspector is where everything about what a deck is lives, and a deck is running material with a time of its own. The mixer could not take it: the mixer holds only what decides how much of each deck reaches the screen, and a strip is 53 pixels wide with nothing spare in it. It sits under the pane's head rather than in it, because the pane's head says which deck is showing and this says what that deck is doing — and because five chips and a deck's name do not fit on one row of a 237-pixel pane.

Sync is one chip that cycles, and the cycle skips. Free, tempo, beat. Tempo scales the rate a deck advances at; beat locks its position, which needs the ability to jump, so it is offered only where the material is closed form and can be evaluated at a moment. Material that accumulates can be run forward and not placed, so beat is skipped rather than shown greyed — and the reason is what the chip says. Tempo is skipped in the other direction, on material that already reads the beat, because scaling its clock as well would make it follow the room twice. Deck A above is on tempo for the first reason and deck B is on beat.

The anchor reads two numbers and asks for one thing, and the offset beside it is the scrub's own value. T128 is the tempo a deck was engaged at, which is what its rate is measured against; B128 +0.25 is that with the deck sitting a quarter beat ahead of the room. A free deck shows neither, because free is the absence of a transport rather than a setting, and a column reading free on every deck would be four words of nothing — so a free deck has no anchor to press either, which costs it nothing, since free is the one mode that never reads an anchor.

Pressing the anchor re-anchors: it asks for the mode this deck is already in. Engaging a mode takes the room's tempo as the reference the material's rate is measured against, so engaging the one that is already running is how an operator says call this the reference tempo — the picture does not move, the rate goes back to 1×, and the scrub goes back to zero with it. It needs no operation of its own; it is the deck's own mode, named. What it needs is a control that can name it, and the chip on the left structurally cannot. A cycle starts at the mode after the one you are on, so the one state it can never arrive at is the one you are in — and the single case where it does land there is material that accumulates and reads the beat, where free is the only mode left and free reads no anchor. That is a fault of the affordance and not a gap in what can be asked for: every control that names a mode outright gets re-anchoring the day it exists, a pad naming one and a call naming one alike, and only a cycle is shut out of it.

Scrub moves by an amount, which nothing else on this panel does — a quarter beat a press, into that same offset, signed and unbounded because a scrub is the one control here meant to go backwards. It is live only where the deck is beat-synced, since that is the only mode that reads the offset, and where it is not the arrows keep their shape and say why. Nothing is shown as a price, because nothing re-runs a history: placing accumulating material would mean running it again from where it started, and that is not an operation this instrument has.

Composite is the deck's, and the renderer chips below are what it turns into a choice. On, a deck's renderers fold into one result and one of them is live; off, they are all overdrawn. A deck publishing a single renderer draws the chip anyway and says that it changes nothing either way, because a deck that grows a second one needs the control already where it was.

Who is holding a control

A bound parameter shows its source instead of a number: a signal, a MIDI control, a sequencer lane, an agent. That readout is where disagreeing with the system begins — a control you cannot see being moved is one you cannot take back — and take back sits right beside it.

A knob is bound to a deck, not to a Set

A MIDI control is learned against the deck and the position in its published interface — knob 3 is knob 3 whatever Set is loaded — which is why the inspector numbers its rows. Binding to a Set's parameter by name would make the mapping a cost you pay again on every swap, and a learn flow exists precisely to make it a cost you pay once. It also settles the crossing: the same Set loaded into two decks is two addresses, because the deck is in the address.

The order is what the Set published — an interface is a list and its order is the author's, which is a small extension of a decision they are already making. Where it published nothing, the order is the Set's own: node by node in the order the nodes run, and inside a node the order its procedure declared them, each key taken where it first appears. That is what the deck above draws — 1 and 2 under L1:0, 3 and 4 under L2:0, 5 under the renderers, and 6 the control that spans two of them. The number follows the interface and not the order the groups are drawn in, which is why 6 is at the top of the pane: a wildcard takes its place among the controls where its key first appears, and it is drawn first because it belongs to no group below. Those are two different questions and the panel answers each in its own terms.

The engine sorted those keys alphabetically until 2026-08-29, and it was right about the thing it was protecting. The keys come out of a hash map, so left alone they come out in a different order on every run, and a console showing its controls in a different order each run is not a console: a MIDI map is worthless against an order that moves. Stable is the requirement. Alphabetical is one way to meet it and it is the wrong one.

It is wrong because the number is read down the pane. Sorted by spelling, this deck's six knobs are amount 1, exposure 2, hue 3, radius 4, turbulence 5, twist 6 — so the left pane reads 2, 4, 5 then 1, 6 then 3, and the one number on this panel whose whole job is to be counted becomes noise. Worse, the order is then an accident of spelling that the Set's author never chose and cannot change without renaming a parameter. Declaration order is the only order that both holds still and means something, and it is the same order the groups are already in.

What it cost the engine was one order kept rather than one derived, and it is paid. Set::published walks the procedures' own parameter lists now and the map of ranges is only looked up. Each node's parameters arrive from its procedure in the order the procedure declares them, and the engine already holds that list per node — it is what a binding is checked against. What loses the order is the map the values are then kept in. So the cost is that the default interface is built by walking those lists in node order and taking each key the first time it appears, in place of sorting the map's keys: no new state, no new thing for a rebuild to restate, and one guarantee added, which is that the numbering does not move when a parameter is renamed at the other end of the Set.

A map is a file — which knob is which belongs to the hardware in the room, so it is saved and recalled per controller and never travels inside a session.

Two focuses, and they do not look alike

The deck selection persists — it is what a key press is addressed to, and it stays put when you tab away into the library. Keyboard focus is transient and goes wherever tab lands. Drawing them the same way would erase which of the two a reader is looking at, so the selection is a solid ring and focus is a dashed one.

Two ways into the selection and neither of them is a control: 0 to 3, and a press anywhere on a strip that no knob under the pointer claimed. The second is why the strip is the one thing in this bay with no capsule and no track of its own to press — it is the whole column, and what it means is address the keys here. A deck the mixer is not drawing a strip for cannot be selected, which is the letter on the library's pill kept honest at the source: a readout that named a deck the press would be refused on would be worse than no readout.

Authority is per node

man / sug / auto on each node head, never a global mode. Manual is yours alone, suggest proposes into the staging lane, auto acts. One of the four properties the whole system is defined by, so it belongs on the face of the panel rather than in a preferences window. A node nobody has spoken for is manual, which is not a placeholder but the only default that is not that global mode thrown for every node by nobody.

Every node takes one, including the ones that look like they would not. The camera takes an authority; so does a field, whose parameters are declared, addressable and yours to ride even though it draws nothing. A node whose knobs something else can move is a node something else can be let at, and that is the whole of the test.

A head that folds more than one node carries no chip, because there is nothing for it to carry. L4 renderers above stands over three nodes. One chip on it would be one of three answers drawn as the answer, and a press on it would set three nodes at once — the switch rule 06 refuses, narrowed from the instrument to a layer but still the same shape, and still unable to say the arrangement that rule calls useful: this renderer is yours and that one is not.

So the chip's place is filled with a reading rather than left empty. man ×2 · sug is what the three under that head are set to. It is not a fourth value — there are three and they are the vocabulary's — and it is not a control; it is a count, the way 4 of 4 in the mixer head is a count. A head that simply dropped its chip would be a control going away without saying why, which is the one thing this panel does not do. A Set with one renderer has one node under that head and the chip is the control it always was, and no fold mark beside it, because a group of one has nothing to open.

The head opens, and that is how a renderer's authority is reached at all. Pressing the mark unfolds the group into one head per renderer — L4:0, L4:1, L4:2, each with its own address, its own chip and its own rows. That is not a second idea of where things live: it is the same node groups the rest of the pane is made of, and the fold is a saving of two heads' height that you hand back when you need an address. The renderers keep their fold by default because the chip row under the head is a choice of one, and that choice is the deck's rather than any renderer's.

The operation is not owed and the route is. Set a node's authority names a deck and a node and exists; what did not exist was a way to name the third renderer while its head was folded, and the fold above is that. The fold itself owes a row on the operations page, beside the other controls that arrange this console rather than the material — and it is named as owed here rather than written from this drawing, which is the way round that keeps a specification a specification. MIDI reaches none of this: a map line carries a deck number, a range, or a word from a closed list, and it cannot spell a node address at all.

The camera is a node, and it is the one with nothing to turn

A camera procedure is a node like any other. It gets a group, an address, an authority chip and rows for whatever it publishes, and nothing about it is special enough to say twice. Neither deck above has one, which is a fact about these two Sets and not a rule.

The rule is the group under it. Every Set has a built-in camera whether or not its files declare one — a node, last among the L3s, called orbit where nobody named it — so L3 addresses something in every Set that exists, and both panes above draw it.

It is drawn with no rows, and not because it has nothing to set. The orbit has six numbers: a radius, a speed, a height, a field of view, and two clipping distances. None of them is a parameter of that node. They are the Set's own, set from outside when the Set is made or loaded and read by the node rather than declared by it — which is why the node publishes nothing, and why a head with a line under it saying so is what a Set's camera honestly looks like from here.

The head is drawn rather than the group omitted, because the head has values. An address, a name, and an authority that a node nobody has spoken for has by default. Leaving it out would make a deck's camera the one part of a deck that the panel where everything about what a deck is lives does not admit exists, and a Set with two cameras would then have one of them appear and the other not, which is worse than both being plain. A control you cannot use says why rather than going away, and a node you cannot reach is the same sentence one level up.

Where an operator reaches those six numbers is: not here, and barely anywhere. There is no flag that aims a camera. A Set file can record two of the six — the radius and the speed — and the other four come back at their defaults, so a camera saved and a camera loaded are not the same camera unless the four were default already. That is a gap this page can name and cannot close.

What is owed is an operation, and it is owed a decision before it is owed a row. The vocabulary names one and leaves what it acts on open, deliberately, because the camera is two things and they are not one operation: the built-in orbit, whose numbers are the Set's and come in through a record, and a camera procedure, whose parameters are written the way any node's are. Which of the two the row means decides whether it carries numbers of its own or should not exist at all. This page specifies what the bay shows and stops there — the group, the head, and the line saying what it waits on — because writing the control from this drawing would be answering that question with a picture.

The beat moves, always

A person does not read a stopped display as current — and on a panel where nothing moves, a panel that has stopped and a panel that is idle are the same picture. So the beat grid is a light that travels: one traverse a bar, every dot as bright as the light is near it, and the two either side of it always adding up to exactly one dot's worth. It is not decoration and it is not spare budget. It is the cheapest thing the console has to say this is live, it needs no legend, and it is the only statement still working when whatever would otherwise report the fault is itself what has stopped.

One dot lighting at a time would say the same thing only between two flips; a light that is somewhere at every instant says it at every instant. The still picture above is a frame of that travel — the instant of a beat, where all of the light is on one dot.

The octave is a person's, and only one half of it is ever live

½ and ×2 move the grid an octave, and they are the one thing about the tempo that no measurement is allowed to decide. The tracker folds every candidate it hears into the window the grid is already in, so a set that started an octave low stays an octave low however long it listens — folding is what keeps a drifting tempo in one octave, and it is exactly what cannot climb out of one. Pressing this moves the grid and the window together, so tracking carries on in the new octave instead of folding straight back. The phase does not move either way: doubling subdivides the beats already there and halving takes every other one, so the beat you can see stays where it is. It is as authoritative as a tap — the run of evidence behind the old octave is thrown away, so the estimates still arriving from it for the next quarter second cannot pull the grid back.

The refusal is drawn rather than reported, because this is one the panel can work out before you press. A move is refused when the result would leave the range the tracker searches, 60 to 200 BPM: outside it there is nothing to lock to, so the next estimate that disagreed would drag the grid back, and a control that undoes itself two seconds later is worse than one that says no. The panel is holding the tempo already, so it can say which press will be refused; a tap that turns out to be a performer still finding the beat can only be reported afterwards, and that is the difference in what the two controls are allowed to promise. Above the row, ×2 keeps its shape and its reason at 128.0, where doubling would ask for 256.

That range is under two octaves wide, so the two halves are never both available — above 120 only ½ is, below 100 only ×2, and between the two neither of them is. It is a control with two faces of which at most one is ever live, which is a thing to know about it rather than a fault in the drawing: the alternative is a single chip whose face changes with the tempo, and a control that renames itself is worse than one that says no twice.

The four things beside it are one group because they need one thing. The pill that says whether a room is being listened to, the latency offset, the tap and the octave all mean nothing without an audio input — the offset is a term in the lead the tracker corrects against, not only a delay on the picture, and the other three write to the tracker outright. So they are drawn at the pills' own tighter gap rather than spread at the row's, with the pill that says whether there is an input at their head, and they sit after the bar rather than among the readouts: the tempo, its label, the beat grid and the bar are one clock and now read as one.

Health, in the transport

Frame time against the budget, and what the last write did — landed, rolled back for cost, or failed to build. The rollback already happens on its own; what was missing was any way to see that it had. Unbreakable on stage is only reassuring if it is visible.

The latency offset, and which offset it is

Between what a room hears and what it sees. It is signed both ways on purpose: which of the two is late is a property of the room, and a PA delayed to the back of a hall puts the sound behind a projector that had looked slow. Held inside 200 milliseconds either way, which is past any single device and well into the region where a whole beat has been mistaken for an offset. Negative and the picture waits for the music, positive and it leads — and the sign is the half that gets read wrong at two in the morning, so the panel says it in words rather than leaving −15 ms to be interpreted.

It is in the transport beside audio‑in, because that is what it depends on: with nothing attached there is nothing for the picture to be early or late against, and the pill next to it is what says whether there is. The value is absolute and the keys are the nudge — five milliseconds a press — because a control that could only be nudged is a control no fader can reach.

Two things on this panel are called an offset, and they are not the same thing. This one is milliseconds and there is one of it for the instrument; the one in a deck head is beats, there is one per deck, and it is where a scrub goes. The unit is what tells them apart, so neither is ever drawn without it.

The arrangement is a file, and the reset is one of them

The pill is the map pill's shape, and that is the argument for the row rather than a resemblance noticed afterwards: a named file with save, load and start a new one under a single control is exactly the family this is — and a new one is the reset. The default arrangement is not a fourth thing beside the three. It is the one you could not have saved, because it reaches code rather than a file; so it has no name, and nothing you file can take its place.

What it saves is what the rest of this panel promises to keep: every fold, every boundary you have dragged, the solo, and the sizes a folded bay and a window dragged too small are both holding for you. What it does not save is the window. An arrangement carries the viewport it was written at and comes back at the one in front of you, which is what lets a console arranged on a laptop open on a projector without a margin round it.

The name is typed and never given, and that decides the shape of the control rather than being a preference for typing. A stamp chosen for you files arrangements under handles nobody can ask for again, and a numbered slot moves the day you delete the slot above it. So load is a list — the names already filed, which a hand picks without typing anything — and save is the one item on this panel that asks for letters. Once a name is in use, saving again means that name: saving over it is what saving it again is. The field has the keyboard while it is up — every key is a letter of the name, including the ones that are panel keys the rest of the time. Return finishes it and esc backs out with nothing saved, which is the one place on this panel where esc does not quit.

A name is letters, digits, - and _, because it is one path component and it names a file under arrangements/. Anything else is refused when you finish typing it, naming the character that stopped it — and it is refused there rather than at the key, which is deliberate: the field takes what you type, and the rule about what may become a file lives where the file is written. A name with a space in it can be typed and cannot be saved, and the panel says which character it was rather than quietly dropping it.

Two more refusals, and they are refusals rather than guesses. A name nothing is filed under is said back to you and the console does not move; an arrangement that disagrees with itself is refused whole rather than repaired into something you did not save. Both are read where the bytes are, not here.

The look is two controls, and they sit where a frame leaves

They are two because they are two asks, not because they are two things. One record carries the operator, the exposure and the white point together, since a stream that set a level without naming the operator it applies to would describe a look nobody can reconstruct. An ask is a different matter: a control change that turns exposure alone has no operator to offer, so the tone map and the level are asked for separately and whoever writes the record fills in the rest from the look that is running. Two asks is two controls, and one of them is a choice from a closed list while the other is a number.

They sit at the end of the left half because that is where a frame leaves. Everything else in this row is already grouped and each group has a reason: the tempo, its label, the beat grid and the bar are one clock; the four beside them all need an audio input; learn, the map and the arrangement are what this console is set up as. A look is none of the three — it needs no input, it is no file, and it is played rather than arranged — so it is a fourth group and it goes last, against the spacer. The tone map is the one transfer the whole pipeline ends in, applied immediately before the single encode at the output, so the row reads in the order the frame does: what the music is doing, what this console is set up as, the last thing done to the picture, and then — across the spacer — what that picture cost.

The tone map cycles and names where it arrived. One press moves it to the next of the four and wraps; what leaves the panel is aces, not next, because a surface that can only step has no way to arrive and two surfaces stepping the same control disagree about where they are. The cycle is this control's, the four names are everybody's, and a pad or a model is offered the four rather than the step. A menu would fit here and it is still the wrong shape: a card is a gesture that outlives the press and takes every pointer event until it is shut, which is a price worth paying for a list of files somebody named and not for four words that never change. The capsule is drawn to the widest of the four whatever is in it, so the control does not move under the name it carries.

Exposure is twelve stops of track and you click where you want it. A sixty-fourth to sixty-four, logarithmic, with 1.00 exactly at the middle: a stop is a ratio, so an additive step is enormous at 0.1 and invisible at 8.0, and a linear track is that same mistake drawn in space — unity would sit a pixel and a half from the left-hand end. The track is 48 pixels for 48 quarter-stops, which is one pixel a press, so pointing at this control reaches exactly the values stepping it reaches and neither surface can ask for something the other cannot. A press sets it outright rather than taking it in hand, which is the one thing here that reads oddly beside the mixer, where a press off a knob deliberately does nothing: a fader has a handle and a value that must not jump under it mid-gesture, and this has neither. There is no knob drawn, for the same reason — a handle nothing can drag is a lie about what the control does.

The figure carries what 48 pixels cannot draw, and it is after the track rather than before it. It is the one part of this group whose width moves with its value, so putting it last leaves the track where it was: a control that walked away from the pointer as you set it would be worse the more precisely you were trying to use it. The number is written the way the Master bay's out is written, because the two are the same kind of reading — and the note below is where they stop being the same number.

Master, and the two levels that are not one level

The bay's out and the tone mapper's exposure are two values, and what makes them two is the chain drawn under the row. out is the level the composited frame enters this chain at; exposure is the level going into the transfer at the other end of it; feedback, bloom and rgb shift sit between them. A frame effect reads what it is handed, so which side of it a level is applied on is the picture: a feedback trail fed at half the level decays from half, and one fed at full level and dimmed afterwards decays from full.

The cost of saying so now is that today they are the same number. With nothing in the chain, out and exposure multiply in two places with nothing between them, and no frame this program can draw distinguishes 0.5 on one from 0.5 on the other. That is the price of the alternative reversed: one value costs nothing today and costs the separation the day the first effect lands, because a value folded into the tone mapper's would have to be pulled back out of it. The engine holds them apart already — the mix applies out where it writes the composited frame, and the present pass applies exposure where it reads that frame — so what is left to land is the chain, not the arithmetic.

A hand moves this fader now, and it is the only route it has. The drag is one operation — Master out, which the operations page names — and it ends in a record like every other control on this panel, so the level a hand asks for and the level a stream would replay are one value. What it still has no route from is a key, a map and a model: no letter is bound to it, no map target names it, and the operations page carries all three as gaps rather than as designs waiting on a control.

The two levels are told apart on this page by how each is reached as well as by where it is applied, and the gestures are opposite on purpose. out is a fader with a handle: you take the knob and it keeps whatever offset you took it at, so the level cannot jump under the hand mid-move. exposure is a track with no handle at all and a press sets it outright, because there is nothing to be mid-way through. Neither can be moving on its own when you reach it — a scheduled move is per deck, and both of these are the deck's own output — so neither has a fade to cancel and neither draws a mark for one.

Sequencer

One row per lane. The row keeps its width and the cells divide it, so eight steps are landscape cells and sixteen are portrait ones. Each lane sits under the mixer strip it drives.

A lane is a fifth route into the vocabulary, and not a binding. It emits operations on the beat, the way the pointer, the keys, a map and a model do — which is the same sentence One vocabulary, four ways in below already made about the sequencer, and this note used to contradict it. A binding cannot be what a lane is, on two counts. Its target is a parameter inside a Set, and three of the four lanes drawn here are deck faders, which are no Set's. And a binding does not yield to a hand: it is a projection recomputed every frame, so a lane built that way would rewrite the fader you were holding sixty times a second — a display that moves when you push it rather than a control. Every hand-driven setter on a deck cancels what was moving it; nothing in the binding path has anywhere to put that cancel. As a route it needs neither: a lane and a hand meet at the same call every other pair of surfaces already meets at.

The step grid is the beat clock subdivided and not a fourth clock — a step is a pure function of the beat, so correcting the tempo changes the rate from now on without moving a beat that has already happened. Two things are worth knowing rather than discovering: a step onset is seen at the next frame, which is right for a sequencer that writes values and would not be for one that fires events; and an eighth at 128 BPM is faster than the band the beat clock's rule was written for.

Library, and staging under it

A scope and a walk, not one flat list: favourites, my sets, app presets, a folder — and the scope list is itself extensible. Staging is where candidates wait, whether they came from you or from an agent; a rejected one costs nothing, because the previous artifact is still in the library.

The staging lane, and the sense in which a candidate waits

There is no regenerate, and there was never going to be one. Generating is a conversation with a model and it happens outside this process — no network and no secrets enter the render process, which is why MCP is the only prompt surface. What crosses the boundary is a node's source, and the operation that takes it is check and write one node's source, which already exists and is already reached by a model. So the lane's first producer is not a new mechanism: it is the write this instrument already performs, with somebody else at the other end of it. The three-step revision — try a parameter, then a range, then a regeneration — only reaches this lane at its third step. The first is write a parameter and lands inside the frame with nothing to stage; the second has no cheaper path here than the third, because a declared range is a line in the source and changing one is a write like any other.

What that costs the sentence above: a candidate does not wait. A write is checked, written, built on a worker and swapped in at a frame boundary; nothing anywhere holds a finished build out of the picture. So the candidate for a node is what that node is playing, and the lane is an A/B over time rather than over space — you are looking at the new one and the one it replaced is one press away. That is by design and not a shortfall: regeneration is destructive in a slot and safe in the library, because every version that compiled is put into the store under its own content address on the way past, whether or not it stayed on screen. A rejected one costs nothing is that fact and nothing more romantic.

A candidate row is four things, and the mock draws a fifth that has no value behind it. The node it is for — L4:0, the address the inspector already writes on a node head. What the procedure calls itself, which is its own proc name, and the artifact's short hash where a file declares no name. Whether it is on screen: landed, rolled back for costing too much, or refused, which is find out what a write did read against one node — a candidate you cannot see is not one you can judge. And when it arrived, formatted by the host and handed in, the way every other derived value in this panel is.

What the lane draws today is three of those four, and the third of the three is not the node. A verdict arrives from the engine as a swap event, and a swap event is about a build: a rebuild restates every node of a slot, so what the engine can say is this slot's new material landed and never this node's did. So the row is addressed by the deck it landed on — the same A to D the preview cells carry, and the only thing naming a deck — and what it carries beside that is the build's own name and the verdict. The node is omitted rather than approximated, and the letter is not standing in for it: the address a verdict has is the slot, and the finer one is a derivation nobody has written. It is derivable in principle — a build can report a hash per node, and consecutive builds differ where the hashes do — but the program that draws this panel reports none of them. Those hashes cross on the build channel only when the build is putting its sources into the store, and this program's watchers are handed no store, so the channel a diff would read is not merely unread: it is silent. That is two missing things rather than one, and only the command-line player has the first of them. Until a build reports its nodes and something diffs consecutive builds, a node written on this row would be invented. The two operations that address a node wait on the same derivation, which is why neither is offered on a row yet.

What is omitted with it is who wrote it, and when. The mock's you, 14:41 and agent split a run into two producers, and this run cannot tell them apart: a hand in an editor and a model over MCP reach the same file by the same path, and the record that would say what instruction produced a version — with the model that answered it and the seed it ran at — has no producer anywhere. The coloured dot goes with it, because the colour is the producer. And the when is not drawn either, which is the one of the four that has a value. What it has not got is a spelling: three exist in this workspace and none of them is the mock's 14:41, so a fourth written for this column would be a second answer to a question the Library's own time column is already waiting on. It is one decision and this bay does not take it twice. This lane omits what has no value behind it exactly as the Library omits its star, and it omits the time for exactly the reason the Library omits its own.

The head's count is not drawn, and neither is the mock's third row. A pill in a bay head on this panel is a control; every readout the mock draws in one is undrawn, and the number here would say what the rows already say — this lane has no truncation to report, where the Library's foot has. The mock's a rejected candidate costs nothing is a note to whoever is reading the mock rather than a thing the lane draws, which the paragraph on the empty lane below says twice over.

Accepting settles the node and changes nothing on screen. Keep a candidate is the operator's verdict and it is not the watchdog's: a version is up there because it compiled and then held the frame budget, which is a judgement about cost, and whether it is the one to keep is a judgement about taste that nothing in this instrument can take. Keeping writes no record — the procedure change was recorded where the swap landed — and the row leaves the lane. There is no control for it yet, so what takes a row off the lane today is the watchdog's own verdict in favour: a version that held the budget leaves the file and the picture agreeing, which is the whole of what the lane is there to show a disagreement in. That is the cost judgement standing in for the taste one, and it is honest about which of the two it is.

What the substitution does not do is empty the lane, and this page used to claim it did. A budget verdict is only ever reached on a slot that is being drawn: a parked or resting slot's trial is frozen, on the rule that a slot nobody is looking at is not paying for the frames it would be judged on, so a build that lands there has a verdict outstanding for as long as the slot stays off air and its row never leaves.

What this page said next was a lane defect, and it was not one. It said that the program hosting this panel opens deck A live and the other three parked, that every slot watches the same pair of files, and that one save therefore rebuilds all four — deck A's row clearing on its thirty measured frames and three rows staying in the lane for the rest of the run. The first and the last of those were true. The middle one was a defect in the program, not a reading of the lane: every deck was handed the two paths typed on the command line, so four watchers polled two files. Each deck runs from its own copy now — see Every deck runs from its own copy below — so a save reaches the one deck whose file it is, and an ordinary run puts one candidate in this lane and clears it on the budget's verdict. The lane was reporting the fault correctly the whole time.

The reading it was reporting is still reachable, and it is still worth having. Load a Set into a parked deck with l, or edit deck C's own scratch file, and a build lands on a slot that is not being drawn: its trial is frozen, its verdict is outstanding, and its row stays until that deck goes on air. That is now something an operator did on purpose rather than the by-product of every save, and the row is the only thing in this instrument that says so. It is still an argument for keep, and a narrower one than this page used to make: the taste-against-cost paragraph above says the watchdog's substitution answers the wrong question, and this says that on a deck nobody is looking at it does not answer at all. The only thing that can take such a row off the lane is a person saying they are done with it — or putting that deck on air — and the first of those has no control yet.

A candidate that was rolled back is the row this lane most needs to draw, because the file and the picture disagree. When the watchdog throws a version out for cost it puts the previous Set back on screen at the t it was parked at — and it does not put the previous file back. So the picture is the old version, the source on disk is the new one, and the next rebuild of anything in that slot swaps the over-budget version in again. Nothing else in this instrument says so, and this row is now what does: it shows the build as rolled back, on the deck it was rolled back on, for as long as that stays true. What it cannot yet offer is the act that mends it — put a node's previous version back names a node, and a node is what a swap event has not got. Keep would not be offered on that row either, nor on one that was refused: there is nothing up there to keep.

And there is one disagreement this lane still cannot show, which is the one it would most like to. Refused on a row is a build that failed — the files checked, and the Set they were assembled into would not build. A .kir the checker turns down never becomes a candidate at all: the diagnostics are printed off the render thread and nothing is requested, so no verdict is emitted and there is nothing for a row to be about. That is exactly a file that disagrees with the picture, and it is said on standard output and nowhere on this panel.

Rejecting is a write like any other, and can itself be rolled back. Put a node's previous version back names the node and not a source: the lane can say not this one and cannot say four kilobytes of IR, so the surface says the part it can say and whoever performs it reads the file. It goes through the same check, the same worker and the same frame boundary, which means a version that was fine a minute ago can come back over budget on a fuller deck and say so. One step, and never a cursor: choosing among older versions is walk the edit history, which is on the operations page with no address anybody has settled — and this lane is the surface that row has been waiting for.

And the version it puts back has to exist, which in this program it does not. What a restore reads is the store's edit history — every version that compiled, kept whether or not it landed, which is why a real attempt thrown out for cost is exactly the one still there. That history is written by a run that was told to keep one, and the program that draws this panel keeps none: nothing is put under a content address and no version is filed, so a restore here would have nothing to restore from. The operations page says this of the same act and this page did not. It is the second half of what the control waits on, the first being the node address above, and neither half is a drawing.

Empty, the lane draws nothing at all. No row, no placeholder, and no standing sentence: with nothing outstanding there is nothing to say, and a body saying so would be a sentence about an absence. The mock's third row — a rejected candidate costs nothing — is a note to whoever is reading the mock, not a thing the lane draws, and it is the reason this paragraph exists. And empty is this lane's ordinary state, which is what makes it different from every other bay here: a library with nothing in it is a library nobody has filled, where a staging lane with nothing in it is simply a run in which nobody has rewritten a procedure yet — which is every run at its start and most runs throughout.

That is the one thing this bay is drawn wrong. Its height is fixed at three candidate rows, so an empty lane holds a hundred and twenty-five pixels of the left pane open over nothing, and it holds them at the expense of the Library, which is the bay in that column that absorbs. The lane's honest height is its contents: the head alone with none waiting, one row at the height that is already written down as this bay's minimum, and the mock's full height at three. The arrangement cannot say that today — it is built once, from constants, and no bay restates its own height — so this is a specification and not yet a reading.

A folder scope reads Sets, and a bundle is not a third thing

The question was whether a folder holds Sets or artifacts, and it had three candidates: a directory of .kir files, a directory of Set files, and a bundle. Two of the three are one thing and the third is out. A Set file and a bundle are the same file — both a .kbset, one record per line, and a bundle is that file with the sources it names appended inside it — so the scope draws one kind of row rather than two. A .kir is not one of them: it is a single node's source, addressed by its content, carrying no layer, no slot, no parameters and no name a person chose. Nothing in the vocabulary takes one. Load material into a deck and read what one Set holds both name a Set the library already keeps, and the one operation that names a file is take one in, which takes a Set. A directory of .kir files is a directory of parts, and a library lists what you can put on a deck.

So a folder row is a take, and the reader for it already draws the line the scope was waiting on. A bundle arrives whole. A Set file with nothing inlined arrives exactly when this library already holds every artifact it names, and where it does not it is refused naming the node that is missing rather than being called a bad file. That is one row, one operation and one refusal sentence for both spellings, which is why the difference between them never has to appear in the scope list: it is a property of a file, not a kind of collection. A fourth candidate arrived after this was settled, and it lands on the same side — a Set file that names its parts by relative path rather than by their content is a Set file, is one row, and is taken in by the same operation. What it adds is a resolution, and that is A Set has two forms, and loading one is packaging it below.

Two limits, and they are what a row from a folder costs whichever of the two forms it is in. A name you already own is refused rather than overwritten, because an id that arrived inside somebody else's file is not the instruction that an id you typed is — so a folder row can meet a Set of yours and ask for a rename. And a folder row is not a row you play from directly: the Set is in the library before it is on a deck. That is one press rather than two because taking it in is what gives it the name the load needs, and it is the same order the scope list is drawn in — a folder is a way in, and my sets is where things are.

What is owed is the asking. List what the store holds carries what a Set holds and which layer it uses, and it has nowhere at all to put a directory — so no operation in the vocabulary can ask a folder for its listing. The chip and the walk under it are specified here and the operation behind them is named as owed rather than written from this drawing, which is the way round that keeps a specification a specification.

Choosing between the chips is a different thing, and it is not owed any more. The operations page carries it as one row — Choose which scope the library shows, one operation over whichever scopes the list holds, because a list that grows while the program is running cannot have a row each. What is still owed is the asking, and it is owed by folder alone: the other three are answered by things that are there when the program starts.

What keeps a favourite, and where it does not travel

The star is a control over a fact nothing in this workspace keeps: no field on a Set listing, no record that carries one, no operation that names one. So what is decided here is where a favourite lives, and it is decided by what happens when a Set is copied to another machine rather than by where it is convenient to write.

In the Set file, a star travels with the material, which makes your attention somebody else's the moment you send one on — and it has to be written: a Set file's own time is what the column beside each name reads, so starring one would push it to the top of a listing ordered by when it was made, and a favourite would be indistinguishable from an edit. In a session, a star is a thing that happened at a moment: a session is written only while the recording is on, so a press with rec off would keep nothing, and a stream that replays would hand the favourite back as an event rather than as something that is true.

So it is kept beside the Sets, in the library itself, and the cost is that a favourite does not travel with a Set. Copy the library and the stars come with it; hand somebody one Set and it arrives unstarred; a Set that leaves here loses its star. That is the right consequence twice over. It keeps a Set file byte for byte what it was, which is what the whole instrument's determinism is measured against and is not a thing to spend on a star. And it keeps favourites a statement about this room rather than about the material — which is also what makes the scope a filter over my sets rather than a fifth place a Set can be, so nothing is in two collections and nothing has to be kept agreeing.

The operation is owed, and the star is drawn as a control anyway. Nothing in the vocabulary names a favourite, so there is nothing yet for a key, a map or a model to reach — and a row invented from this drawing would be the specification written backwards. What this page settles is the half a control cannot be specified without: the value it reads, and where that value lives.

Where the presets come from, and why it is told rather than found

A program cannot ask the system where it is, so the honest answer is an argument. POSIX gives a running program no reliable way to learn its own path — what the platforms offer is a best effort under a different name and with a different way of failing on each of them, and the one thing every program really is handed is the word its caller chose to start it with. So the root of the shipped library is --presets DIR: something said to the program rather than something it works out. The alternative is what this program does today, and it is why this scope has never had a file in it — the pair it opens on is resolved against the tree it was built in, baked in at compile time, so a binary that leaves the machine that built it points at a directory that is not there.

Nobody types that flag on an installed copy, and the packaging is what supplies it. Every platform has some way of naming a place relative to where the program was installed — a bundle's own resources, a prefix's share directory — and using it is the packaging's job rather than the operator's. The flag is what makes it sayable at all, and it is the same shape as --store DIR, which names where what you keep is written. Neither of them is a second way to name what plays: they say where this program's data lives, and what goes on a deck is still two paths and nothing else.

With nobody saying, four places are looked at in order and the first that is a library wins — a directory with a .kir in it, which is not the same question as whether something is there, and the difference is not pedantry: a build of this program from source sits in a directory that already has an examples beside it, full of object files and no presets. Each place is relative to the running binary, because that is the one location a program can come close to: ../Resources/examples beside it, which is a macOS .app; then ../share/karakuri/examples, which is a unix prefix install; then examples in its own directory, which is a folder somebody copied. The fourth is the workspace tree baked at compile time, and it is named here rather than hidden. It is what keeps a run out of the repository behaving as it always did; it is last, so no installed copy reaches it before the three above it; and it is asked the same question as the rest, so on any machine that is not the one that built the binary it simply fails and the search ends. Which of the four answered is said at startup, and it is read back off the answer rather than written down beside it.

A path you typed is yours, and finding none is not a fault. A --presets naming a directory that is not there is refused, naming the flag, rather than quietly replaced by one of the four: a path an operator typed is an instruction, and doing something else with it while saying nothing is the failure this instrument spends everywhere else to avoid. And when nobody said and nothing was found there is no preset library — the program says that once, naming --presets, creates nothing to make the sentence less true, and this scope lists nothing. An empty tier is a library nobody has filled rather than something gone wrong; the one place it is more than that is the pair this program opens on when you have named none, which comes out of the preset library and so is not there either, and that is refused loudly — naming the flag, and saying the two paths may be given instead — rather than opening a window onto nothing.

Every deck runs from its own copy

The two paths on the command line are not what any deck is watching. Before the window opens, this program copies that pair once per deck into <store>/scratch/ and points each deck's watcher at its own copy. The directory and a line per deck are printed at startup, and those are the files to open in an editor — the line says the directory, then deck A: A0-drift_shell.kir + A1-soft_points.kir, deck B: B0-drift_shell.kir + B1-soft_points.kir, and so on for C and D. Editing the two paths you named on the command line moves nothing.

The name carries the deck and the node's place — the letter the preview cells are drawn with, the node's index in that deck's Set, and the file's own name. A library load writes into the same directory under the same spelling, which is where this rule was written down first: <store>/scratch/<name>.kir overwrites what is there, so two decks whose material happens to share a name would silently become one file and the second load would move the first deck on its watcher's next poll.

Four decks on one preset are four files, and that is the requirement rather than the price of it. A slot is the unit that gets rebuilt, judged and swapped — that is what it means for each deck to have its own hot-swap — so a deck whose file is also another deck's cannot be moved on its own. Sharing one file meant one save rebuilt all four decks, put four candidates in the Staging lane, and left the three parked ones with a verdict that could never arrive. It also meant the four decks this instrument opens with, which exist to be driven apart, could not be.

Why a panel copies at all is the same reason the command-line player does: before there was a scratch, an editing surface was given write access to whatever path the material came from, and three shipped presets were replaced in one session — recoverable only because they happened to be in version control, which is not a property of this program. Nothing is ever written where the presets are.

This program has no read-only run, which is why the copies are made unconditionally rather than behind a flag. The command-line player copies only for --watch or --mcp, because an offscreen render should be a function of its arguments and must not leave a directory behind. Every deck here is built over a file watcher, changing material is editing a file and letting the worker build it, and l writes into this same directory on a key press. So the store directory exists from startup on a run that saves nothing — the same cost karakuri-cli --watch already pays. It is not the cost the Library bay declines: that bay refuses to create a store in order to list one, and this is a run that has already decided to write.

A Set has two forms, and loading one is packaging it

A Set file names its parts in one of two ways, and which of them you are looking at changes what a load does. A .kset names them by relative path and lives beside them, which is the only way a directory of parts can be told which of them go together — nothing in a part says what it belongs with, and nothing in one may. A .kbset names them by the content of the parts themselves, and it is what this store holds and what travels. They are not two formats so much as one file and that same file resolved.

So loading a .kset is packaging it. The parts it names are read off the disk beside it, filed in the store under the address of their own bytes, and the Set is written into your library as a .kbset. That is the same work send a Set to somebody does, which is why taking one in is not a second row on the operations page: it is one operation performed at two moments, ahead of time when you are sending and at the press when you are not. What a load owes on top of it is a refusal — a part named from outside the file's own directory is not read, and the path is said out loud, because a Set somebody handed you is not a way of asking this machine for its files.

The store holds the resolved form and only that one, and the reason is the swap. A Set lands on a frame boundary, and one that cannot hold the frame rolls back on its own onto what was playing — which works only because there is nothing left to work out at the moment it lands. Let the other form into the store and a load walks the disk while the deck is changing over: a neighbour missing, or moved, or changed since the file was written, and the failure arrives after the exchange has begun, in the one place this instrument is built to be unable to fail. So the reading and the hashing happen when you take the file in, in front of you, with nothing on air. The extension is what makes that checkable by reading a name, rather than by opening every file the library lists and hoping nobody copies another one in afterwards.

Which is why opening a preset leaves one of your own behind. What ships is a directory of parts and the .kset files that say which of them go together, so loading a preset is a packaging step, and a packaging step writes into the store: my sets gains a row you did not make. That is how making it yours works — it is the same copy-on-load a run that can be edited already performs on its material — and it is what keeps the shipped library the read-only thing this page promises, because nothing is ever written where the presets are. It is said here so that nobody meets it as a surprise: the first symptom, otherwise, is a library filling up with everything you have ever tried.

The two names differ by one letter in the middle, which is the hardest place there is to see one. Sorted by name, night-b.kset and night-b.kbset sit against each other and are the same word in a hurry, and this page is not going to pretend you will tell them apart at a glance. What it claims is narrower: a store cannot contain a .kset at all, so the two are in one directory only in an authoring tree of your own, and wherever mistaking one for the other would cost something the program refuses rather than your eye deciding. The b is what has been done to the file — it has been bound — so the pair reads as one word with a mark on it, which is what they are. The cost is real, and it is paid in ls rather than on the panel.

How a Set reaches a deck

The operation exists and is called load material into a deck. It names a deck and a Set, which is exactly the two things this bay and the mixer already carry between them, so what was missing was never the operation but the route.

The pill names the deck selection, so both operands are already on screen. The cursor in the list says which Set; the selection says which deck — the same value x reads, and the reason the selection persists while your hands are in the library instead of following focus. A load is then a cursor and a key with no pointer anywhere in it, which is rule 01's constraint met by the arrangement rather than by a second control. That is why the constraint was allowed to decide the question: the answer that satisfies it needs nothing added, and the answer that does not — a destination chosen in the library's own foot — would be a third selection on a panel that already has to explain two.

The letter on the pill is the whole warning. Deck A above is selected and on air, so the pill reads load → A and a press would replace what the room is watching. Nothing refuses it: what may be asked for is the instrument's to decide, and a panel refusing what the key allows would be a second rule kept in a second place. What the control owes instead is to say where it lands before the press, and the letter is that. Select the deck you are preparing and the letter follows you.

Dragging a row onto a strip is a second route to the same command, and never the first. It is worth having because it names both operands in the one gesture, which makes it the only way to load a deck without selecting it first; it is second because a route the keyboard cannot take would break rule 01 on its own. Two ways in, one name — which is the only reason a drag is safe to add later.

The key is l, and the choice is the operations page's rather than this one's, because which keys exist is that page's to say. It was free on both keyboards — the panel bound nine keys and the command line thirty-nine, and l was in neither — so nothing had to be taken off anything to spell the one gesture this bay exists for. MIDI is empty for a reason of its own rather than for want of a letter: every target a map can name carries a deck number, a range, or a word from a closed list, and a Set is none of the three. A map belongs to the hardware in the room and this list belongs to the store, so a line naming a Set would be a line about something the map has no reason to know — and the same argument that keeps a map a file is what says a Set id does not belong in one.

The cursor moves on the arrow keys and gets no row on the operations page, which is a decision and not an omission, and it is the one place this bay parts company with the deck selection beside it. Select a deck has a row because the selection is what every deck-addressed operation's keyboard translator fills its deck in from — a gain, an opacity, a blend, a residency, a sync, a scrub and a crossfade are unreachable from the keyboard without it. The cursor is read by exactly one operation, and that one carries the Set id in its own payload. So the three surfaces that are not the keyboard have nothing to reach: a map cannot name a Set at all, for the reason above; a model says load this Set into this deck and has no use for a pointer at a listing it is not looking at; and the panel's route is the drag, which names both operands in the one gesture. A row here would be the first rule written down as a permanent gap — three columns that can never be anything but empty, on an operation that exists to move a highlight. Keyboard focus is the precedent, and it is settled under Two focuses, and they do not look alike above: a pointer nobody but the keyboard can move is the surface's own, and this page is where it is specified.

What the press does to the slot is re-point its source, and that is the whole of what this row was blocked on. The obvious reading — build the Set and put it in the slot — is the one thing that may not happen: Deck::install is the function that does it and is deliberately unreachable from a key or a surface, because a live run changes its material by letting the worker build it, and the worker is what the budget watchdog is attached to. A Set handed straight in is a Set nothing measured, on a deck that cannot roll it back. So the press writes the library Set's procedures out where the slot's watcher is already looking and lets go: the worker reads them, compiles them off the render thread, and the swap lands at a frame boundary and is judged for thirty frames against the budget like every other build. A Set that cannot hold the frame is rolled back on its own and the deck goes back to what it was playing, at the t it was parked at. That is not a special case written for the library; it is the path an edit already took, and the library is now one more thing that walks it. The staging lane under this bay says what happened, on the deck it happened to, because a load is a build and a build is what that lane is about.

Program, sized by height

A 16:9 view filling a wide centre column would be 763 pixels tall and eat the inspector whole. So you drag its height and it letterboxes into the width it has — which is what the engine already does. Height given back is rows in the library and the inspector, which are the two bays that take it.

The bay is two regions and they fold apart. The picture is a sink, listed in Outputs as program view, and it is on screen exactly when that sink is on — so there is no state where it is hidden and still costing a pass. The deck previews under it are auditions of their own, so they stay when it goes.

The picture carries no label of its own. The bay head already says Program, and this is a region somebody may be capturing: a capture that is neither the canvas nor a clean crop of it is worse than useless, and a word burnt into the corner is exactly that. The A–D under it keep their letters, which are outside anything you would capture and are the only thing naming a deck.

The picture is the canvas's shape, and it is the console that makes it so. It is as large as the region allows and centred in it, which is the rule a deck preview cell already follows — so the rectangle is a clean crop rather than something to crop, and the bars that used to be rendered inside it every frame are not rendered at all. Drag the program's height and the picture grows with it, because the height is what you are given to answer how big should the picture be.

Widen the window far enough and the four deck previews move beside the picture — A and B down the left, C and D down the right — because a wide bay leaves ground down each side and the picture can have it instead. Whichever arrangement leaves the larger picture is the one you get, so it flips once on the way out and once on the way back, at the same width both times: nothing is remembered, and a window dragged wide and back comes back to exactly what it left. The cells get larger when they move, not smaller, and each keeps its letter where it was — C is at the top of the right-hand side because it was third in the row, and it stays there whether or not C is running. Fold the picture away and they go back under it: there is nothing for them to be beside.

What is beside it is the bay, and a capture pays for that. The leftover is the Program bay's own card, not black. Solo the program view and the window is that region, so a window whose shape is not the canvas's captures panel-coloured bars where black would read as an ordinary letterbox — size the window to the canvas and there are none. That is what a is for at the engine's own window, and this one has not got it yet.

What a model is refused, and where a class opens

Four indicators, one per class: this bay's head, the Mixer's, the Master's, and the Outputs row, which has no head of its own and takes the pill beside the word that stands in for one. Each opens one class of operations to a model, and each starts shut. The list a model is offered never shortens. Every operation in the vocabulary is connected to it, a shut class included; what shut changes is that the call is answered with a refusal instead of being performed. That is the harder of the two mechanisms to build and it is the one worth building — a tool a model cannot see is not a tool it was refused, it is a tool the first rule says exists and this surface says does not.

Shut, the pill is the ordinary capsule and reads mcp · shut; open, it reads mcp · open and is drawn armed — the same .pill.armed treatment the transport's audio‑in pill carries, for that pill's own reason. A pill that only changed colour would leave which class unanswered, and a pill that only changed its word would make a shut class and an open one look alike at the distance a panel is read from; the word answers the first question and the treatment answers the second, and a class is both questions at once. Every one of the four is drawn shut above because every class starts shut — open is that same pill after the press its tooltip promises, and never a second indicator beside it.

A refusal can say why and an absence cannot. A model that finds no tool learns nothing, plans around a hole it has no name for, and reports back that this instrument cannot do a thing it can do — the same failure as a frame that comes out plausible and wrong: nothing breaks, nothing is logged, and the person who could have fixed it with one press never hears about it. So a refusal names the class, says an operator can open it, and says which bay the pill is in. The person sitting beside the model is the one who can act on that, which is who it is written for as much as the model.

The toggle belongs to the map rather than to the instrument. Every surface reaches the vocabulary through a layer that says which gesture names which operation — the ⊕ MIDI: line every tooltip here ends with is that layer, one control at a time — and whether a surface may reach a class of operations at all is the same layer's second job. This pill is not a control over the mixer or over the program, then: it is the same editor as the icon in a tooltip, entered from the other end, per class instead of per control. It is also why the opening is not an operation of its own. The first rule would make it reachable from all four surfaces, and a permission an actor can grant itself is not a permission.

What a shut class costs a model is a plan it finds out about on the fourth call. The list it read at the start of a session is still true at the end of one, by construction, so nothing announces a class being shut while a model is three calls into something: it learns by being refused, and the refusal is the whole of what it has to work from. Whether anything should be pushed rather than discovered that way is not settled here. Neither is whether one press opens a class or a single operation of it, whether an opening outlives a restart, or whether an open class shuts itself again after a while. Nor is whether a bay carrying no class draws the pill at all — the Library, the Inspector, Staging and the Sequencer have none, and an indicator that is present everywhere reads as a state wherever it is absent.

Outputs

Every place a composited frame goes, as one switchable list. A projector window is a second sink and lives in the repository; Syphon, Spout and NDI are plugin sinks and appear only when a plugin is loaded. Fullscreen is just what an application does. The picture in the Program bay is a sink like any other and is the first row.

All of them may be off, and that is a state worth having. The deck previews are auditions rather than outputs, so with every sink off you can still see the decks and work on them — which is what setting up before doors is. Nothing is refused here, so nothing has to be explained: a control that quietly declines the last of something is a rule an operator can only find by experiment.

Every icon explains itself

Density is only earned if it is legible on demand, so hover says three things: what the control is, what state it is in, and what a click will do. The third is the one usually left out, and leaving it out is what makes a compact panel unlearnable — the reader sees the state and never learns it is a control. The tooltip also carries the control's MIDI assignment, which is how learning one works: point at anything with a tooltip and move a knob. Coverage then takes care of itself — a new operation gets a tooltip, and a tooltip is a thing a map can reach.

One vocabulary, four ways in

Every operation is reachable from the GUI, from the keyboard alone, from a mapped MIDI control, and from MCP. That is structural before it is visual: each operation is named once, and every surface routes into that name. An operation only the mouse can reach breaks it; so does one the map cannot address. The sequencer is a fifth route, and it only works for the same reason.

GUI → command keys → command MIDI map → command MCP → command sequencer → command

It is checkable rather than merely intended: with the vocabulary exhaustive, a test asserts that every command carries a key binding, is addressable by the map, and is what the GUI emits. And it is half-built — L4:1:exposure is already one spelling shared by --param, --publish, the MIDI map and the MCP tools. So this milestone opens with naming, not drawing.

What a surface routes through is a map, and one surface has one. A map is the layer between a gesture and the vocabulary, and it does more than one job: which gesture names which operation, whether that surface may reach a class of operations at all — the mcp pill is that second job, per class instead of per control — and the helpers that carry a musical instant into a record. Only the MIDI surface has that layer as data today. The keyboard's bindings are written into the program, the panel's are the derivations that draw each control, and MCP's are the names of the tools it publishes. So none the map cannot address is a rule about one surface and an intention about three, and it is said here rather than in the seven rules because it is the map's gap and not the vocabulary's.

Still open

Where the tracker's own doubt goes

The estimator can already say it thinks the grid is at half the music's tempo — it is x2? on the status line the program prints, shown only when the estimate behind it is worth trusting — and it is the thing that tells a performer to reach for ×2 at all. The panel draws the control and not the prompt, which leaves the operator to notice for themselves the one thing the instrument already knows. It needs a home in the transport, and it needs deciding next to a second fact: a grid at half the music's tempo can only be doubled below 100 BPM, so above that the hint and the refusal would arrive together.

A layout, not a specification — it is what the implementation is checked against. The mechanisms behind the mixer, the inspector, the library and the outputs are built, and so are the Master bay's out and the staging lane's row; the three effects of the master chain, the sequencer, the two acts a candidate row would offer, authority and the panel itself are not. The seven rules are what all of this obeys.
