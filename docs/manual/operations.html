Karakuri · the manual

Every operation, and every way in

The first rule says every operation is reachable from the panel, from the keyboard alone, from a mapped MIDI control, and from a model over MCP. This page is that rule written out one operation at a time — which makes it two things at once: what the instrument can do, and where it does not yet hold.

How to read a row

built — this surface reaches it today designed — this surface is meant to reach it and does not yet nothing — this surface cannot reach it at all

The key column is the instrument's keyboard — the keys cargo run -p karakuri binds, and no other program's. Two programs in this repository have a keyboard and they are not the same keyboard: the instrument binds sixteen keys, karakuri-cli binds thirty-nine, and eight letters mean one thing at the panel and something else at the command line. Five keys agree, and four of the five arrived together: esc quits on both, and 0 to 3 select a deck on both, because a deck is a slot number and there was nothing to translate. So a built key badge names a key an operator at the panel can press today, for the same reason a built panel badge names a control they can reach: both columns are about the one program a player launches, and a badge that mixed the two would tell a reader to press a letter that does something else in front of them. A designed key badge names the key this page has specified — the console defers to it, because which keys exist is this page's to say — and the instrument does not bind it yet. The command line's own keyboard is printed by cargo run -p karakuri-cli -- --help, which is derived from its key handler and checked against it; it is not this column, and a second copy of it here would be a list that goes stale.

64 operations, and 53 of the 272 ways in exist. Eight of them are the console's own shape — moving a boundary, folding a bay or a pane, bringing one back, solo, and the three that reset, keep and put back an arrangement — and nine of their thirty-two routes exist: the drag, a key on each of five, and the transport row's arrangement pill on three. Every row of that section has a route now, and the two that carry a name were the last: a key press cannot type one, so keeping an arrangement and putting one back waited on a control that could, and the pill is it. Resetting is the default case of those two and is reached from both — r, and start a new one on the same pill. Twenty key badges are designed rather than built, and nineteen of them are keys the command line binds today. The column is the instrument's keyboard and the instrument binds sixteen keys, so the nineteen are what the command line reaches and the panel does not; the letters stay because this page is where a key is specified, and seven of them are already spoken for at the panel — f and g fold, z brings back what is folded, u unsolos, r resets, p asks the panel to report itself and n changes its colours. Whether the instrument takes those letters or the command line gives them up is a decision nobody has made, and it is a change to this specification rather than a note on it. The twentieth is e, and no program binds it — the library's scope row is specified here before either keyboard has it, which is the order everything else on this page arrived in. Fifteen panel routes are marked built, and they are the whole of that column: the Mixer bay's six — the strip trim, the strip fader, the strip button, the tally chip, the strip mask mini and the strip itself, which is where a deck is selected — every divider on the panel, which drags; the transport row's tone map capsule, its exposure track and the arrangement pill's three rows; the deck head's sync chip and its scrub; and the Master bay's out. The Mixer's first five already emitted these operations while the console was an example; what changed is that cargo run -p karakuri puts them in front of a player, which is what a panel badge claims and what no program did until it existed. The sixth is the newest and is not a control at all but the strip's own body: a press anywhere on it that no knob claimed selects that deck, which is the one route this bay had for a value nothing on the panel kept. The one that is not a control is the console's own shape: a press on a gap takes the boundary in hand, and the moves and the release after it are the panel's rather than the interface's over it. The Outputs dot is drawn and pressable and its badge stays designed, which is a distinction this page owes rather than an oversight: the dot folds the program view away and brings it back, and this row names choosing where the frame goes across a switchable list of sinks. One entry of that list is not the list, so the control performs part of what the row names and the badge would be an over-claim. Beyond those fifteen, 46 more are designed and out of reach, most of them in bays nothing draws yet, and three the panel cannot reach at all. Where a badge names a region, that is where the control lives in the console; three name none yet, and naming one is a change to this specification rather than a note on it. Three of them are named here for the first time: the arrangement family — reset it, save it, put one back — lives in the transport row, beside the pill that names the MIDI map in use. That pill is already the shape this family needs and the reason the row is the right one: a named file, with save, load and start a new one under a single control, and a new one is exactly what the reset is. All three are built now, and the order they were built in is the order this repository works in: these badges specified a control that did not exist, the page that owns the console's words drew it next, and the panel was built to that. Nothing was designed at the keyboard. The Master bay's out is the fourteenth and it arrived by the same order read from the other end: the console page has drawn that fader since before anything could move it, the engine gained the level and the one multiply next (the console's Master, and the two levels that are not one level), and this row is the last of the three rather than the first. Its other three columns are empty and that is the state rather than an omission — no key is bound to it, no map target names it and no tool publishes it. Eight rows are new and nothing reaches any of them yet: the master chain's three effects, and the five things an operator does to a pattern. They are the two bays the console draws and this page named no operation for at all, which is the one way a bay can be missing from a count of badges — a bay with no rows cannot have a designed one, so it read as finished by not being asked. Each names where its control lives and carries nothing in the other three columns, and what they wait on is not the same thing: Feedback waits on which cut of the previous frame it reads, the sequencer's five on a pattern, which nothing in this program holds, and the other two effects on the chain that would run them. The counts here are read off the code rather than estimated, and they are what the Interface milestone is for.

A launch badge means the only way in is a flag, before the run, and nothing can change it afterwards. On the grid means the change is scheduled and lands on a musical instant rather than when you asked for it.

Decks

What is resident, what is on air, and what material is in each.

Select a deck

immediate

Which deck the keys are addressed to. A console pointer — it writes no record, and it is not the same as keyboard focus. The panel draws it as the solid ring round a mixer strip, and every other control that names a deck without being pointed at one reads it: the load pill's letter is this selection, and the crossfader spans it and the one after it. The four keys are the command line's four, which is the one thing the two keyboards agree on besides esc — a deck is a slot number, so nothing had to be translated for the panel to mean what the shell already meant.

panel click a strip key 0 1 2 3 MIDI — MCP —

Put a deck on air, prime it, or take it off

immediate; priming is a request

Three states, not two switches: live is stepped and composited, priming is stepped out of sight and adds nothing to the mix, allocated is held — compiled, buffers kept, its t where it stopped, so a deck taken off and brought back resumes rather than restarts. Live is honoured: nothing demotes a deck that is on air. Priming is a request the governor reconsiders every pass, and a deck asked to prime with no room is parked — the request stands and takes effect when there is room, so what the console shows is the residency the deck reports, not the one it was asked for. MIDI names the deck and the state, so it needs no selection and no notion of which way to flip — a pad per state, and a key acts on the selected deck.

panel tally chip key space, w MIDI note → residency N live|priming|allocated MCP —

Load material into a deck

on a worker

A load re-points the slot's source; it does not hand a built Set to a slot. That was the whole of what this row was blocked on, and it was smaller than it read: Deck::install is still the one function that puts a Set in a slot and is still deliberately unreachable from a key or a surface, because a live run changes its material by letting the worker build it — which is what the budget watchdog is attached to. So l writes the library Set's procedures out where the slot's watcher is already looking, and everything after that is the path an edit already takes: polled, compiled off the render thread, swapped at a frame boundary, judged for thirty frames against the budget, and rolled back on its own if it costs too much. A Set that cannot hold the frame does not take the room down with it, and the staging lane says so on the deck it landed on. The two operands are on screen before the press: the cursor in the library says which Set, the deck selection says which deck, and the pill in the library's foot reads load → A so the letter warns you where it lands. The panel badge is the drag from a row onto a strip — a second route to the same command, naming both operands in one gesture — and it is not drawn. MIDI is empty for a reason of its own: every target a map can name carries a slot, a range or a word from a closed list, and a Set id is none of the three.

immediate

Master out

immediate

One level on the composited frame, at the entry to the master chain — the whole fold rather than one deck of it, which is why this is the row in the group that names no deck. It is not the exposure below it. That is a second level at the far end of the same chain, and what makes them two is where each multiplies: this one is applied where the mix writes the frame, the exposure where the present pass reads it, and feedback, bloom and rgb shift go between them. A frame effect reads what it is handed, so which side of it a level sits on is the picture — a feedback trail fed at half the level decays from half. With the chain empty they are indistinguishable in every frame this program can draw, and that cost was taken deliberately rather than met later: the console page's Master, and the two levels that are not one level is the argument. Unbounded above 1.0 and floored at zero, on the trim's terms — the pipeline is linear HDR and nothing has tone mapped it yet. Nothing can schedule a move on it: a transition is per deck and this is the deck's own output, so there is no fade for a hand on it to cancel.

panel master out key — MIDI — MCP —

Feedback

immediate

The chain is fixed, and that decides what these three rows are. The master chain is presets, all of them loaded, in the order the console draws them — feedback, then bloom, then rgb shift, between the Master out above and the Exposure below. Nothing here edits the chain, switches one effect off or adds an effect somebody wrote: those are controls the panel does not draw and decisions nobody has taken. What each of the three names is the operator moving that effect's parameters — the figure the bay draws beside its name — and a finer surface than that is not this milestone's. Where a chain an operator has set up is kept is settled and is not a row here: it is library data in two tiers, on the same shape as a saved arrangement, with the presets shipping beside the material and the operator's own under the store. What such a file contains cannot be written down while the chain does not exist, which is why no row here lists a parameter.

Feedback is the exception, and what it waits on is an answer rather than a control. Reading the previous frame is a cycle, and the previous frame is not one thing: it could be a Set's output, the raw frame the mix wrote before anything downstream touched it, or the frame as it stands after some effect in this chain. So a feedback procedure has to name which cut it reads, and nothing in the vocabulary can say that. A cut that is read has to be held, which is a frame-sized target and a copy per frame — and holding every cut against the chance that something reads one is the cost nobody would pay, so what this points at is the selection recomposing the pipeline: a cut nothing reads is not retained, and naming one adds the retention to the graph the way an edge adds a pass. Nothing builds a graph that way today. This row is here because the bay draws the effect, and it is the one row in the group whose badge is empty for a question rather than for want of work.

panel master chain key — MIDI — MCP —

immediate

It runs in linear HDR, before the one tone map, and that is the whole reason it sits on this side of the transfer — what it blooms from is unbounded light, where the same effect after the tone mapper would bloom from a displayable approximation of it. It reads what it is handed, so the level it blooms from is the Master out's and not the exposure's, which is what makes those two levels two.

The sequencer

What an operator does to a pattern. What a lane does to a deck is already on this page.

Toggle a step

immediate

A lane is a fifth route into this page and not a binding, and that is why this section is as short as it is. A lane emits operations on the beat the way the pointer, the keys, a map and a model emit them — so the three lanes the console draws first are deck faders emitting Opacity, which the mixer already constructs, and the fourth writes a Set parameter, which is Write a parameter. A lane needs no new operation to drive anything. What needed naming is the other half, and it is these five rows: what a hand does to the pattern.

This one is the cell grid — a step of one lane is on or it is off, and the change is heard the next time the playhead reaches that step rather than when you asked for it. What it waits on is a pattern, which nothing in this program holds; that is why the bay draws nothing below its head. Where a pattern is kept is settled — library data in two tiers, presets shipping beside the material and the operator's own under the store, on the same shape as a saved arrangement — and what a pattern is is not, so this row names a step and cannot yet say what it is a step of. The grid under it is no new clock: a step is the beat clock subdivided and a pure function of the beat, so correcting the tempo changes the rate from now on without moving a beat that has already happened.

panel sequencer lane key — MIDI — MCP —

Mute a lane

immediate

The pattern is kept and drives nothing, and the control is the lane's own label — so silencing a lane costs the row no width and the pattern nothing. It is not Take a parameter back, and the resemblance is worth refusing where somebody would otherwise reach for it: that operation stops a signal driving a knob and it names a deck and a parameter inside that deck's Set, where three of the four lanes drawn are deck faders, which are no Set's. It could name the fourth lane and not the other three, and an operation that reaches one lane in four is not this row. So the mute is a lane's, addressed the way a lane is addressed, and what that spelling is waits on the same pattern the row above waits on.

Choose a pattern's steps and what a step is worth

immediate

The head's three pills — 16, 1/8, 2 bars — and they are not three settings. Sixteen steps of an eighth apiece is two bars, so any two of the three fix the third, and the panel shows all three because an operator reads a pattern in whichever of them they are counting in. Choose which scope the library shows

immediate

One operation, and the scope is what it names — not a row for each chip, because the row of chips is not a list this page can finish. A scope is added while the program is running, so a vocabulary carrying one operation per scope would go short the moment an operator points the bay at a directory. Nor are the four the console draws four different acts. Favourites is that page's own words for this library filtered rather than a fifth place a Set can be, so choosing it and choosing my sets differ in the question asked and not in what is asked. Presets is a second root, and one the program is told — --presets DIR, or whatever the packaging put beside the binary — rather than one it works out, so nothing has to be built for it to be shown that is not there at startup. And folder is a root somebody names during the run, which is the only one of the four short of anything at all: what it waits on is an operation that can ask a directory for its listing, and that is List what the store holds's gap — that row has nowhere to put a directory — rather than this row's. Choosing which library is being read is one act whichever of them is chosen, and what differs between them is what the listing after it costs.

The two empty columns are empty for two different reasons, and neither is want of a control. A map line carries a slot number, a word from a closed list or a range, and this list is the one thing about the library that is not closed: it grows when a directory is added, and a map belongs to the hardware in the room where the scopes belong to the store — the same refusal Load material into a deck meets over a Set id. A model has no chip to press and no listing in front of it to point at: what it wants of this row it gets by naming the library in the listing it asks for. The key is e, and it steps to the next scope and wraps. A bare press cannot type a name and here it does not have to — the scopes are a short row of chips in front of you, so stepping says which by showing you, and a list that grows is still reached by stepping through it. The letter is the one letter of scope neither keyboard has spent: s solos at the panel and prints the status at the command line, and c, o and p are the command line's. So the letter is free on both keyboards — which is what l was chosen for one row up — and this is the one designed key badge on this page that is not a key the command line already binds.

Send a Set to somebody, and take one in

not a run

Every inlined source must hash to the address its slot names before anything is stored, and an id already taken is refused rather than overwritten. The way in takes both spellings: a .kbset that arrived whole, and a .kset that names its parts by relative path, whose parts are read off the disk beside it and filed under the address of their own bytes before anything reaches the store — and a part named from outside that file's own directory is refused, out loud, because a Set somebody handed you is not a way of asking this machine for its files.

Taking one in is not a second row, and a preset is what shows why. Packaging is one operation at two moments — ahead of time when you are sending, and at the press when you are not — so loading a Set out of presets or out of a folder is this row performed at the second of them, and the console reaches the same answer from its own side: a .kset resolved into the store is the work this row does, which is why my sets gains a row you did not make every time you open a preset. Which is where this row's panel column stands. The taking-in half has a specified control already and it is not one the library still owes: it is a row under presets or folder, loaded, which is Load material into a deck's route and is drawn on the console today. What nothing anywhere draws is the sending — writing one out to hand somebody — so the badge stays designed and names the bay rather than a control, for the reason the Outputs dot's does: a control that performs half of what a row names would make the badge an over-claim.

Keep a candidate

immediate

The operator's verdict on a version, and it is not the watchdog's. A version is on screen because it compiled and then held the frame budget over thirty measured frames — that is a judgement about cost, and whether it is the one to keep is a judgement about taste that nothing in this instrument can take. Keeping it settles the node and clears its row from the staging lane; nothing about the picture changes, because a write does not wait to land and the candidate is already what the deck is playing. It writes no record — the procedure change was recorded where the swap landed — which is why the words here are not the engine's accepted, rejected and rolled back: those name a build's cost verdict, and the lane is the one place both verdicts are read at once. The lane draws its row now and this control is not on it: the row is addressed by the deck a build landed on, because that is what a swap event carries, and this names a node — which is a derivation nobody has written. Until it exists, what clears a row is the watchdog saying the version held its budget.

Put a node's previous version back

on a worker

What a rejected candidate costs nothing is made of. Every version that compiled is kept under the store's edit history — gated on compiling rather than on landing, so a real attempt that was rolled back for cost is exactly the one still there — and this puts the one before the current version back through the same check, build and frame-boundary swap any other write takes. One step, never a cursor: choosing among older versions is walk the edit history, which has no address anybody has settled. It names a node and not a source, because a lane can say not this one and cannot say four kilobytes of IR — the surface says the part it can say and whoever performs it reads the file. It does write a record, at the swap, like every other procedure change. The row it belongs on is drawn and this act is not offered on it, for the reason above and for one more: the versions it would put back are the store's edit history, and the program that draws this panel keeps none.

What the gaps say

MIDI is the narrowest by a wide margin. It reaches eight of these 64 operations, and it cannot express a node address, a parameter name or an id — nor a bare number, which is why it reaches one half of the mask and not the other. A map line carries a slot number, a word out of a value list, or a range, and an angle is none of the three; a pad naming a shape alone would have to invent the angle beside it, and the map reads nothing back to invent it from. So the front has a fader and Set a deck's mask shape stays empty here rather than being given a route that guesses. That is settled and not outstanding: the two ways to fill the badge in are a number the format cannot check and an angle somebody defaults, and the thing that would decide between them is a control that shows an angle, which does not exist yet. It no longer has a vocabulary of its own, though: a map line names one of the operations on this page, and it names it outright — which state, which mode — rather than saying step it. It also has no idea of a selected deck — it names the deck in the message — where the command line's keys have nothing but the selected deck. The same operation is addressed two different ways on two surfaces.

Nineteen of the keyboard's twenty designed rows are one program's keys waiting for another's. This column is the instrument's keyboard, and nineteen rows on it — across the transport, the decks, the mixer, the library and the window — are reached by pressing a key today, in karakuri-cli, which is not the program the panel is in. That is a gap of a kind none of the others here are: nothing is undecided, nothing is unspellable, and no control is unbuilt. The keys exist, they are specified on this page, and the window a player opens does not read them yet. The twentieth row is not one of them: e steps the library's scope and neither program binds it, so it is a key waiting on both rather than on one. What it waits on is the work of putting those operations behind the panel's own keyboard, and one decision beside it: which of the two keyboards keeps the seven letters they both want.

A model can rewrite a whole procedure and cannot turn one knob. MCP edits material, reports swaps and reads the library, and touches nothing in the mix, the clock or what is on air. It can make a deck unrecognisable and cannot put it on air.

Authority is named here and reachable from nothing, and it is short of something none of the other empty rows are. It is not waiting on a decision: rule 06 settled the address, and it is per node. It is not waiting on a control either — the node head it belongs on is specified on the console page, like every other panel route on this page. What it waited on was the deck, and the deck arrived. A node's authority has to survive every rebuild, the way that node's parameters and bindings already do, and a rebuild that let it be worked out afresh would hand a node back to an agent an operator had taken it from, saying nothing. A rebuild restates them now. What is missing is a writer — nothing sets one, so nothing has to survive yet, and the row is a word the instrument can keep and has never been told. That is a shorter list than the one this paragraph used to name, and naming the old blocker after it closed is how a row stays unbuilt for a reason nobody rechecks.

Arranging the console is reached from the keyboard, and until the instrument existed it was reachable from a pointer and nothing else — which is what this paragraph said, and it called it the first rule broken by the surface the first rule is about. Five of its eight rows are built in the key column and four in the panel column: f and g fold whatever the pointer is over, z brings everything back, s and u solo and unsolo, r resets, and the pointer has the drag and the arrangement pill's three. How a region is unfolded without a mouse is decided, and z is the answer: a folded region still has no rectangle, so a pointer still cannot reach it to undo itself, and it no longer has to. What is still short is the boundary. A divider is dragged and nothing else, because Layout::set_divider takes a viewport pixel and a key press cannot mean one — the vocabulary carries Move a boundary as undecided for exactly that reason. It is the one row here with no route but the pointer's, and it is what is left of the first rule being broken by the surface the first rule is about. Reset the arrangement is no longer waiting on the family it is the default case of in order to be reached at all — r performs it — but MIDI is still empty: it names no region, so a pad could reach it the way a pad reaches tap, and none does.

The two rows that carry a name were empty in every column, and a name is why. Keeping an arrangement and putting one back each carry a name the operator picked, and for as long as no surface could say one, no surface could reach them. The panel can say one now — it is the only one of the four that can — and the other three columns are still empty for the reasons below rather than for want of a control. A bare key press cannot type a name and there is nowhere on this console to show the operator a name it picked for them, so a keyboard route would file arrangements under stamps nobody could ask for again — and putting one back could not be bound at all, because nothing at a key press says which. A map line carries a slot number, a word from a closed list or a range, and a name is none of the three, which is the same refusal Load material into a deck meets over a Set id. MCP is the one surface whose grammar has no objection: it already spells a Set id, an arrangement name is the same shape, and the tools are simply not published. What these two waited on was one control, and it is built. The panel badges said where it went — the transport row, beside the map pill, which is already a named file with save, load and start a new one on it — the console page drew it, and the panel reaches all three rows now. It typed the name, which is what the other three surfaces still cannot do, so those columns are unchanged and empty for the reason above rather than for want of a control.

Two bays are drawn and reach nothing, and their rows are the newest on this page. The master chain and the sequencer are specified here for the first time, which is the order this repository works in rather than an admission: a control is named on this page, drawn on the console, and built after that. What separates these eight from the other unbuilt rows is that none of them is waiting on a control. The chain's three effects wait on the chain — giving L5 a writable form is the console's phrase for what would have to exist, and nothing in the engine folds one. The sequencer's five wait on a pattern, which is authored state nothing in this program holds; where one is kept is settled and what one holds is not. And feedback is the sharpest of the eight, because it is not short of work at all: until somebody says which cut of the previous frame it reads, and what holding that cut costs, there is nothing to build, and a row drawn in this specification and absent from the panel is what the milestone this page meters cannot close over.

The command line is the only way into most of what a Set is — parameters, bindings, edges, the published interface, capacity — and it is only open before the run starts. Reachable from nothing at all while running: parameter writes, binding changes, edge rewiring, the camera, seeds, capacity, canvas size, un-compositing a deck, folding every renderer back in, a gain fade, and starting or stopping a recording.

Counted from the source, not from this manual. Where a row here and the program disagree, the program is right and this page is a bug — the seven rules are what both are meant to obey.
