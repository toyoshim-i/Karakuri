Karakuri · the manual

What the words mean

Six ideas, and the rest of the manual assumes them. None is complicated on its own; what is worth reading is how they fit — because the shape they make is not the shape most visual software has, and reading the console with the wrong shape in mind is the one way to find it confusing.

A procedure

The smallest thing Karakuri keeps. A procedure is code, not pixels and not a recording — a short program that runs on the graphics card every frame, written in this project's own language and saved as a .kir file. It declares what can be turned on it, and that declaration is what the console reads to know which knobs to draw.

A procedure has a kind, and the kind says what it is made of rather than what it looks like. L1 is the material: what the elements are and how they move. L2 deforms them. L3 is a camera. L4 draws. Field is a distance function the others can ask questions of, which is the one kind that draws nothing at all. And L5 folds several drawn results into one, which is what the mixer itself is.

A procedure is stored by the hash of its own text, so the bytes are its name. Two identical procedures are one stored thing, and a procedure can never be quietly altered under a name that already meant something else.

A Set

One picture's worth of material: a few procedures wired together, with the values their knobs are turned to, the element counts they run at, and the random seeds that decide what they look like. A Set is what you keep and what you load — the unit the library is made of.

Sets hold more than one of most kinds. Two geometries with a morph between them is one Set; one geometry drawn three ways is one Set with three renderers. Which node feeds which is written down rather than inferred from the order you typed them, so reordering a command line cannot silently change the picture.

A Set says what it publishes. A procedure may declare twenty-five parameters and a Set decides which few are worth a knob on the console — and that published list, in order, is also what a MIDI control counts when it is learned. Publishing chooses what is shown, never what is reachable: nothing about it locks an operator out of a control the Set did not think to offer.

A deck

A place a Set is loaded and played from. There are four, and they are mixed together into what you see. A deck is not a layer in an image editor — it is running material, with its own time, that keeps running whether or not it is on screen.

That is why a deck has a residency rather than a visibility. Every deck runs, at the room's tempo, whatever its residency — what a residency decides is what reaches the screen, not whether the material is moving. Live is reaching the screen. Priming is off the screen and warming up, asked for, so that cutting to it does not arrive cold — material that accumulates over time looks wrong for its first seconds, and priming is what buys those seconds back. Allocated is off the screen with nobody having asked. The two off-screen states do the same thing; the difference is that one of them was asked for. Both are in their own preview cell, running, which is what you judge them by.

The mix

How much of each deck reaches the screen. Each deck has a gain, which is a trim on the level its colour arrives at, and an opacity, which is the fader across the blend. They are not two names for one thing: opacity at zero silences a deck under every blend mode, and gain at zero does not — a black card still covers what is under it. The fader is the one your hand lives on; the trim is set once and left.

A deck also has a blend mode, which is how it meets what is beneath it, and a mask, which is what shape of the frame it reaches at all.

The beat grid

A clock the whole instrument shares, driven by an audio input, an external source, or its own oscillator. It matters because changes can be scheduled onto it: a crossfade asked for halfway through a bar starts on the bar, so what you play lands where the music is rather than where your hand was.

Two different requests hide under the word sync. Tempo-sync changes the rate a deck advances at, and following it only ever means taking more or fewer steps — so it is offered to anything except material that is already following. A procedure that reads beats is on the room's grid by its own hand, and scaling its clock as well would make it follow twice; that one is refused, and says so. Beat-sync locks the position, which needs the ability to jump — so it is offered where material can be evaluated at a moment, and refused where material has to be arrived at by running it, since re-running a deck's whole history to correct it is not a feature.

A record, and why everything is one

Every change to what is playing is written as a line in a stream: a fader moved, a deck put on air, a procedure swapped in. Nothing changes the running state except by a record — which is what makes a performance replayable frame for frame rather than approximately, and what makes a saved Set a projection of the same vocabulary rather than a second format that has to agree with the first.

It also decides what a save is. Keeping a deck writes the material on screen — the versions running, at the values they are at — not what any file on disk happens to say. A procedure you wrote and that was rolled back for costing too much is on disk and not on screen, and a save saves the screen.

The shape, in one paragraph

Procedures are code that draws. A few of them wired together with their settings is a Set, which is what the library holds. A Set loaded into a deck is running material with its own time and its own residency. Four decks are folded together by the mix, and everything that changes is scheduled against the beat grid and written down as a record. The console is a surface over exactly that and nothing else — which is why every operation can be listed and counted.
